What Is Probability?

How likely is it, really? Coin flips, dice rolls, weather forecasts — the math of chance, explained simply.

8 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE CALCULATOR INSIDE

You check the weather app and see "70% chance of rain." You roll a die and hope for a six. You buy a lottery ticket knowing the odds are tiny. Probability is the language behind all of it — and it's simpler than it looks.

Probability is a number that measures how likely an event is to happen. It sits on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), and everything in between represents some degree of uncertainty. When you say "there's a 50-50 chance," you're already doing probability.

In this guide, we'll cover what probability means, the key terms you need, the formula for calculating it, the difference between theoretical and experimental probability, and plenty of real examples — plus an interactive calculator you can play with right on this page.

CONCEPT 01

Chance on a scale

Probability is always between 0 and 1. 0 means impossible, 1 means certain, and 0.5 means an even chance.

CONCEPT 02

The simple formula

P(event) = favorable outcomes ÷ total possible outcomes. That's the core of almost every probability calculation.

CONCEPT 03

Theory vs. reality

Theoretical probability predicts what should happen. Experimental probability records what actually did happen.

What Is Probability?

THE BASICS

Probability is a branch of mathematics that puts a number on uncertainty. Instead of saying "it might rain" or "I probably won't win," probability lets you say "there's a 70% chance of rain" or "the odds of winning are 1 in 14 million."

That number always falls between 0 and 1, inclusive:

  • 0 — the event is impossible. Rolling a 7 on a standard six-sided die has probability 0.
  • 1 — the event is certain. Rolling a number between 1 and 6 on a standard die has probability 1.
  • 0.5 — an even chance. A fair coin landing on heads has probability 0.5.

Key idea

Probability doesn't tell you what will happen. It tells you how likely each outcome is. A 70% chance of rain means rain is more likely than not — but it still might not rain.

The Probability Formula

THE CORE EQUATION

For equally likely outcomes, probability is calculated with one straightforward formula:

P(event) = favorable outcomes ÷ total outcomes Both numbers come from the sample space — the full list of what can happen.

That's it. If you can count the total possibilities and count how many of them match what you're asking about, you can calculate probability.

Example: Rolling a die

What's the probability of rolling a 4 on a fair six-sided die?

  1. Total outcomes: 6 (the numbers 1 through 6)
  2. Favorable outcomes: 1 (just the number 4)
  3. Probability: 1 ÷ 6 ≈ 0.167, or about 16.7%

Example: Drawing a card

What's the probability of drawing a heart from a standard 52-card deck?

  1. Total outcomes: 52
  2. Favorable outcomes: 13 (there are 13 hearts)
  3. Probability: 13 ÷ 52 = 0.25, or 25%

Theoretical vs. Experimental Probability

TWO WAYS TO MEASURE CHANCE

There are two main ways to arrive at a probability, and they don't always give the same number at first.

Theoretical probability

This is what the math predicts should happen, assuming everything is fair and equally likely. The formula above gives you theoretical probability. For a fair coin, the theoretical probability of heads is exactly 0.5.

Experimental probability

This is what actually happened when you ran the experiment. If you flip a coin 100 times and get 48 heads, the experimental probability is 48 ÷ 100 = 0.48.

The law of large numbers

With only a few trials, experimental probability can be far off from the theoretical value. But as the number of trials grows, the experimental probability tends to get closer and closer to the theoretical one. That's why 10 flips can give 70% heads, but 10,000 flips almost always lands near 50%.

The Complement: "Not" Probability

THE OPPOSITE EVENT

The complement of an event is everything that isn't that event. If the probability of rain is 0.7, the probability of no rain is 1 − 0.7 = 0.3.

P(not A) = 1 − P(A) The probabilities of an event and its complement always add up to 1.

This is useful when it's easier to calculate the chance of something not happening. For example, the probability of rolling at least one six in four rolls is easier to find by calculating the probability of rolling no sixes at all, then subtracting from 1.

Real-Life Examples of Probability

PROBABILITY IN THE WILD

Probability isn't just for dice and cards — it's used constantly in the real world.

  • Weather forecasts: "70% chance of rain" means that in similar conditions, it rained about 70% of the time.
  • Medicine: A treatment that works in 9 out of 10 patients has an experimental probability of 0.9.
  • Insurance: Companies use probability to estimate how likely accidents, illnesses, or claims are for different groups.
  • Sports: A basketball player shooting 80% from the free-throw line has a probability of 0.8 of making the next one, based on past data.
  • Games and apps: A "1% drop rate" on a rare item means the probability is 0.01 per attempt.
  • Quality control: A factory checks a sample of products to estimate the probability of defects across a whole batch.
Important

Probability describes long-run likelihood, not certainty about a single event. An 80% free-throw shooter will still miss 1 in 5 — that's exactly what the probability says.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What is probability in simple words?+

Probability is a number between 0 and 1 that tells you how likely something is to happen. A probability of 0 means it can't happen, 1 means it definitely will, and 0.5 means there's an even chance — like a fair coin landing on heads.

What is the formula for probability?+

The probability formula is P(event) = number of favorable outcomes ÷ total number of possible outcomes. For a fair six-sided die, the probability of rolling a 4 is 1/6 because there is 1 favorable outcome out of 6 equally likely total outcomes.

What is the difference between theoretical and experimental probability?+

Theoretical probability is what should happen based on math — like 1/2 for a fair coin landing on heads. Experimental probability is what actually happened when you tried it — like getting 48 heads out of 100 flips, which is 0.48. With more trials, experimental probability usually gets closer to the theoretical value.

Can probability ever be greater than 1 or less than 0?+

No. Probability always falls between 0 and 1, inclusive. A value of 0 means the event is impossible and 1 means it is certain. Anything outside that range means there was a mistake in the calculation.

What does it mean when probability is written as a percentage?+

Probability written as a percentage is just the decimal form multiplied by 100. A probability of 0.25 is the same as 25%, and 0.5 is the same as 50%. Weather forecasts use percentages because most people find them easier to read than decimals or fractions.

What is a real-life example of probability?+

A weather forecast saying there is a 70% chance of rain is a probability. So is a doctor saying a treatment works in 9 out of 10 patients, or a game showing a 1 in 1000 chance of a rare item drop. Probability is used any time someone puts a number on how likely something is.
