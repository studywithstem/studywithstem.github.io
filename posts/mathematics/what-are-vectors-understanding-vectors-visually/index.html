What Are Vectors? Understanding Vectors Visually

Magnitude and direction, scalar vs vector, tip-to-tail addition, components — all in one visual guide.

8 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE DIAGRAMS INSIDE

Wind pushes a sailboat sideways. Gravity pulls a skydiver down. A plane flies east while the jet stream shoves it north. Every one of these is a vector at work — and once you see them, you can't unsee them.

A vector is a quantity that has both a magnitude (how much) and a direction (which way). That's it. But that simple addition — direction — changes everything about how you add, subtract and analyse these quantities compared to ordinary numbers.

This guide walks through what vectors are, how they differ from scalars, how to add and subtract them visually, and how to break them into components. Every concept comes with a diagram you can see and, in two cases, interact with.

CONCEPT 01

Magnitude & Direction

A vector is an arrow: its length is the magnitude, its tip points the direction.

CONCEPT 02

Scalar vs Vector

Scalars have only size. Vectors have size and direction — speed vs velocity, mass vs weight.

CONCEPT 03

Addition & Components

Vectors add tip-to-tail, and any vector can be split into x and y parts.

What Is a Vector, Really?

MAGNITUDE + DIRECTION

Think of a vector as a directed arrow. The arrow's length represents its magnitude — how strong or how large the quantity is. The direction the arrow points represents its direction. Change either one, and you've changed the vector.

In written form, vectors are usually bolded or given an arrow: v or v⃗. Their magnitude is written as |v| or just v, and is always a non-negative number.

v = (x, y) A 2D vector is often written as an ordered pair: x = horizontal component, y = vertical component.

For example, a force vector of 10 newtons pointing 30° above the horizontal is a single vector — not two separate things. Its magnitude is 10 N. Its direction is 30° from the x-axis. Both pieces are needed to describe it fully.

Key idea

Two vectors are equal only if they have the same magnitude and the same direction. A vector pointing north with length 5 is not the same as one pointing east with length 5, even though both have magnitude 5.

Scalar vs Vector: The Critical Difference

ONE NUMBER VS TWO PIECES OF INFORMATION

A scalar is fully described by a single number — its magnitude. Temperature, mass, time, speed, energy and distance are all scalars. You don't need to say "20°C pointing north." Temperature is just 20°C.

A vector needs both magnitude and direction. Velocity, acceleration, force, displacement, momentum and weight are vectors. Saying "50 km/h" is a speed (scalar). Saying "50 km/h due north" is a velocity (vector).

SpeedVelocitySpeed tells you how fast; velocity tells you how fast and in which direction.

Here's the practical consequence: you can add scalars like ordinary numbers — 5 kg + 3 kg = 8 kg. But you cannot add vectors like that. A 3 N force east plus a 4 N force north is not 7 N. It's 5 N pointing northeast. Direction changes the math.

Vector Addition: Tip-to-Tail

RESULTANT VECTOR

The standard way to add two vectors visually is the tip-to-tail method. Draw the first vector. Then place the tail of the second vector at the tip of the first. The resultant — the sum — is the arrow drawn from the tail of the first vector to the tip of the second.

Order doesn't matter. Adding A then B gives the same result as adding B then A. This is called the commutative property, and it works for any number of vectors.

Real-world example

A plane flies east at 200 km/h. Wind blows north at 50 km/h. The plane's actual velocity over the ground is the vector sum of those two — about 206 km/h pointing slightly north of east. This is why flight paths need vector correction.

Vector subtraction

Subtracting a vector is the same as adding its opposite. A − B equals A + (−B), where −B is the same length as B but points exactly the opposite way. So the tip-to-tail method still works — just flip the second vector before you add it.

A − B = A + (−B) Reverse the direction of B, then add tip-to-tail.

Components: Breaking Vectors Apart

X AND Y PARTS

Any 2D vector can be split into two perpendicular components: a horizontal part (x) and a vertical part (y). This is one of the most useful skills in physics and engineering, because components are just numbers — and numbers are easy to add.

If a vector has magnitude r and points at angle θ from the positive x-axis, its components are:

x = r · cos(θ)    y = r · sin(θ) r = magnitude  ·  θ = angle from positive x-axis

Going the other way — from components back to magnitude and direction — uses the Pythagorean theorem and inverse tangent:

r = √(x² + y²)    θ = tan⁻¹(y / x) Magnitude from components, angle from the ratio of y to x.

Components turn vector addition into simple arithmetic. Break each vector into x and y parts, add all the x parts together, add all the y parts together, then rebuild the resultant from those two sums. That's exactly how the interactive diagram above works under the hood.

Where Vectors Show Up in Real Life

Vectors aren't abstract math — they're the language engineers, pilots, game developers and physicists use every day.

  • Navigation: GPS and flight planning combine velocity vectors with wind vectors to find the actual path over ground.
  • Video games: Every 3D game engine uses vectors to track position, velocity, and the direction a character or camera is facing.
  • Structural engineering: Forces on a bridge are vectors — beams must handle both the push (magnitude) and the direction of each load.
  • Sports analytics: A soccer ball's trajectory is a vector sum of initial kick velocity and gravity's acceleration.
  • Machine learning: Data points are vectors in high-dimensional space; similarity between vectors tells you how alike two items are.
A quick sanity check

If you walk 3 km east, then 4 km north, you've walked 7 km total — but your displacement from the start is only 5 km, pointing roughly northeast. Distance is scalar addition; displacement is vector addition. They give different answers, and both are correct for different questions.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What is a vector in simple words?+

A vector is a quantity that has both a size (called magnitude) and a direction. Velocity, force and displacement are vectors — you can't fully describe them without saying which way they point.

What is the difference between a scalar and a vector?+

A scalar has only magnitude — like temperature, mass or speed. A vector has both magnitude and direction — like velocity, force or displacement. Speed is a scalar; velocity is a vector.

How do you add two vectors together?+

Place the tail of the second vector at the tip of the first. The resultant vector is the arrow drawn from the tail of the first to the tip of the second. This is called the tip-to-tail method, and the result is the same regardless of the order you add them.

What are vector components?+

Components break a vector into perpendicular parts, usually along the x and y axes. A vector with magnitude r and angle θ has x-component r·cos(θ) and y-component r·sin(θ). Components make vector math much easier.

Can a vector have a negative magnitude?+

No. Magnitude is always zero or positive — it's a length. A negative sign in front of a vector flips its direction by 180°, but its magnitude stays positive.

What is a real-world example of vector addition?+

A plane flying east while wind pushes it north is a classic example. The plane's velocity vector and the wind's velocity vector add tip-to-tail to give the actual velocity over the ground.
