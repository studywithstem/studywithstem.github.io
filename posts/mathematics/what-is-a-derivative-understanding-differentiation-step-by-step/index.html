What Is a Derivative?

Understanding differentiation step by step — the slope of change, explained visually with limits, rules, and a live interactive explorer.

8 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE DIAGRAMS INSIDE

Speedometers, profit curves, population growth — they all hide the same mathematical idea. A derivative is the tool that measures how fast anything changes, and differentiation is the process of finding it.

Calculus has two great ideas: one measures how things add up (integration), and the other measures how things change. The second one — the derivative — is the focus of this guide. It's the math behind speed, slope, and instantaneous change, and once it clicks, you'll see it everywhere.

CONCEPT 01

Instantaneous rate

The derivative tells you how fast a quantity is changing at a single instant — not an average, but right now.

CONCEPT 02

Slope of tangent

Geometrically, the derivative is the slope of the line that just touches a curve at one point.

CONCEPT 03

Limit of a ratio

It's defined as the limit of the average slope as the two points on the curve get infinitely close.

What Is a Derivative? The Big Idea

RATE OF CHANGE

A derivative is a function that describes the rate of change of another function. If you have a function that tells you where something is (position), its derivative tells you how fast it's moving (velocity). If a function tells you the total cost of producing x items, its derivative tells you the cost of producing one more.

At any point on a curve, the derivative gives you the slope of the tangent line at that point. A steep tangent means rapid change; a flat tangent means the function is momentarily pausing.

The core idea in one sentence

The derivative of a function at a point is the instantaneous rate of change of the function at that point — the slope of the curve right there.

The Limit Definition: Where Derivatives Come From

FOUNDATION

To understand differentiation, you need to see where the formula comes from. Start with two points on a curve and find the slope of the line between them:

Average slope = [f(x + h) − f(x)] / h This is the slope of the secant line connecting two points separated by a distance h.

As h gets smaller and smaller, the secant line gets closer to the tangent line. In the limit as h approaches zero, you get the exact slope at a single point — the derivative:

f′(x) = limh→0 [f(x + h) − f(x)] / h This is the limit definition of the derivative, also called differentiation from first principles.

Try it below: move the point on the curve and watch the secant line (dashed) collapse into the tangent line (solid) as the gap h shrinks.

Slope of tangent (derivative) 4.0

The function shown is f(x) = x². Its derivative is f′(x) = 2x, so the slope at x = 2 is exactly 4. Drag h toward zero to see the secant line become the tangent.

The Power Rule: Your First Differentiation Shortcut

CORE RULE

Computing limits every time is slow. Fortunately, there are rules that let you differentiate quickly. The most fundamental is the power rule:

d/dx [xⁿ] = n · xⁿ⁻¹ Multiply by the exponent, then subtract one from the exponent.

Here are a few examples side by side:

x²2x2 · x²⁻¹ = 2x
x³3x²3 · x³⁻¹ = 3x²

Quick check

What's the derivative of x⁴? Multiply by 4 and subtract one from the exponent: 4x³.

Beyond the Power Rule: Sum, Product & Chain

TOOLKIT

Real functions are rarely just a single power of x. Here are the essential rules that let you differentiate almost anything you'll meet in a first calculus course.

Constant multiple & sum rules

  • Constant multiple: d/dx [c · f(x)] = c · f′(x). You can pull constants out.
  • Sum rule: d/dx [f(x) + g(x)] = f′(x) + g′(x). Differentiate term by term.

Product rule

For two functions multiplied together:

(f · g)′ = f′ · g + f · g′ "First derivative times second, plus first times second derivative."

Quotient rule

For one function divided by another:

(f / g)′ = (f′ · g − f · g′) / g² "Low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared."

Chain rule

The chain rule handles composite functions — a function inside another function:

d/dx [f(g(x))] = f′(g(x)) · g′(x) Differentiate the outside, keep the inside, then multiply by the derivative of the inside.

For example, the derivative of (3x + 1)² is 2(3x + 1) · 3 = 6(3x + 1).

Three Ways to Read a Derivative

The same derivative can be interpreted physically, geometrically, and algebraically. All three views are useful.

Where Derivatives Show Up in Real Life

  • Physics: velocity is the derivative of position; acceleration is the derivative of velocity.
  • Business: marginal cost and marginal revenue are derivatives of cost and revenue functions.
  • Biology: population growth rates are derivatives of population size over time.
  • Engineering: finding maximum stress, minimum material, or optimal angles all rely on derivatives.
  • Machine learning: gradient descent uses derivatives to minimize error and train models.
Key takeaway

A derivative is a function that measures change. Differentiation is the process of finding it. The limit definition is its foundation; the rules (power, product, quotient, chain) are the practical shortcuts.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What is a derivative in simple terms?+

A derivative measures how quickly something changes. It tells you the instantaneous rate of change of a function at any point — the slope of the tangent line at that point. If a car's position is a function, its derivative is the speedometer reading.

What does differentiation mean?+

Differentiation is the process of finding a derivative. You take a function and apply rules (like the power rule) to produce a new function that describes how the original one changes.

What is the power rule for derivatives?+

The power rule says that the derivative of xⁿ is n·xⁿ⁻¹. Multiply by the exponent, then subtract one from the exponent. For example, the derivative of x³ is 3x².

Why do we need limits to define a derivative?+

A derivative is the slope between two points, but with the two points infinitely close together. Limits let us describe that "infinitely close" idea precisely: the derivative is the limit of the slope as the gap between the points approaches zero.

What are some real-world uses of derivatives?+

Derivatives are used to find speed from position, to maximize profit or minimize cost in business, to model population growth, to design curves in engineering, and throughout physics to relate force, velocity, and acceleration.

What is the difference between a derivative and a slope?+

A slope is a number that describes how steep a straight line is. A derivative is a function that gives the slope of a curve at every point — it's the slope of the tangent line, which changes from point to point on a curve.
