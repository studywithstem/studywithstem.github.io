How Does the Human Heart Work?

Four chambers, four valves, one relentless rhythm. A visual guide to the pump that never takes a day off.

8 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE DIAGRAMS INSIDE

Your heart beats about 100,000 times a day, moving roughly 7,500 litres of blood through a network of vessels long enough to circle the planet twice. Here's how that actually works — in plain language, with the diagrams to prove it.

The human heart is a muscular pump roughly the size of your clenched fist, sitting slightly left of centre in your chest. Its job is simple to state and remarkable to execute: keep blood moving in one direction, without pause, for your entire life.

Four rooms — two atria on top, two ventricles below — that fill and empty in a precise, repeating rhythm.

The Four Chambers: Rooms With Very Different Jobs

ATRIA & VENTRICLES

The heart is divided into left and right sides by a muscular wall called the septum. Each side has two chambers stacked vertically, giving four in total. The upper chambers are the atria (say: AY-tree-uh); the lower chambers are the ventricles.

Atria are the collection rooms — thin-walled, low-pressure, they receive blood and pass it down. Ventricles are the pump rooms — thick-walled, high-pressure, they push blood out. The left ventricle is the strongest chamber in the heart because it has the farthest to push.

Why the left side is stronger

The right ventricle only pushes blood a short distance to the nearby lungs. The left ventricle must push blood all the way to your brain, fingertips and toes. Higher distance and resistance demand higher pressure — and higher pressure demands a thicker muscle wall.

The Four Valves: One-Way Doors for Blood

NO BACKFLOW ALLOWED

Every chamber exit has a valve. Valves are flaps of tough, flexible tissue that open when pushed from one side and snap shut when pressure changes direction. They ensure blood only ever moves forward.

There are two types. Atrioventricular (AV) valves sit between the upper and lower chambers. Semilunar valves sit at the exits of the ventricles, where blood leaves the heart.

  • Tricuspid valve — between the right atrium and right ventricle. Has three flaps.
  • Pulmonary valve — at the exit of the right ventricle, leading to the lungs.
  • Mitral valve — between the left atrium and left ventricle. Has two flaps.
  • Aortic valve — at the exit of the left ventricle, leading to the aorta and the body.

That familiar lub-dub sound your doctor listens for? That's not the heart muscle contracting. It's the sound of valves closing — "lub" is the AV valves shutting, "dub" is the semilunar valves snapping closed a moment later.

The Cardiac Cycle: One Heartbeat, Step by Step

DIASTOLE & SYSTOLE

A single heartbeat is called the cardiac cycle, and it happens in two main phases. During diastole, the heart muscle relaxes and the chambers fill with blood. During systole, the muscle contracts and squeezes blood out.

At a typical resting heart rate of 75 beats per minute, one full cycle takes about 0.8 seconds — roughly 0.5 seconds of filling, 0.3 seconds of pumping.

As heart rate rises, diastole shortens first — the heart spends less time resting between beats.

The journey of one drop of blood

Follow a single drop of oxygen-poor blood from the moment it returns to the heart:

  1. Enters the right atrium through the vena cava.
  2. Passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle.
  3. The right ventricle contracts, pushing blood through the pulmonary valve.
  4. Travels to the lungs via the pulmonary artery, picks up oxygen, and releases carbon dioxide.
  5. Returns to the left atrium through the pulmonary veins.
  6. Passes through the mitral valve into the left ventricle.
  7. The left ventricle contracts powerfully, pushing blood through the aortic valve into the aorta.
  8. Travels through the body, delivers oxygen to cells, and returns to step one.

Two Loops, One Heart

The heart actually runs two separate circuits at the same time. The right side handles the pulmonary loop — a short trip to the lungs and back. The left side handles the systemic loop — a much longer journey to every tissue in the body and back again.

Try it yourself

Place two fingers gently on the inside of your wrist or the side of your neck. Count the beats for 15 seconds, then multiply by four. That's your resting heart rate — the number your cardiac cycle is built around.

The Heart's Built-In Pacemaker

The heart doesn't need instructions from the brain to beat. It has its own electrical system that fires in a precise sequence, beat after beat.

The signal begins in a small cluster of cells called the sinoatrial (SA) node, often called the heart's natural pacemaker. From there, the impulse spreads across the atria, reaches the atrioventricular (AV) node, and travels down specialised fibres to the ventricles. This delay at the AV node is deliberate — it lets the atria finish emptying before the ventricles contract.

  • SA node — sets the base rhythm; fires 60–100 times per minute at rest.
  • AV node — the relay station; introduces a short pause before the ventricles fire.
  • Bundle branches and Purkinje fibres — fast conducting pathways that spread the signal through the ventricle walls.

Heart Rate Zones: What the Numbers Mean

Your heart rate responds instantly to what you're doing. At rest, it's slow and steady. During exercise, it climbs to deliver more oxygen to working muscles.

  • Resting (60–100 bpm) — normal adult range.
  • Moderate activity (50–70% of max) — comfortable, conversational pace.
  • Vigorous activity (70–85% of max) — harder breathing, shorter sentences.
  • Maximum (roughly 220 minus your age) — the ceiling your heart can sustain briefly.

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

How does the human heart work in simple terms?+

The heart is a muscular pump with four chambers. The right side receives oxygen-poor blood from the body and sends it to the lungs. The left side receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs and pumps it out to the entire body. Valves keep blood moving in one direction, and a natural pacemaker sets the rhythm.

What are the four chambers of the heart and what does each do?+

The right atrium collects oxygen-poor blood returning from the body. The right ventricle pumps that blood to the lungs. The left atrium collects oxygen-rich blood returning from the lungs. The left ventricle pumps that blood out to the body. The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate the highest pressure.

What is the cardiac cycle?+

The cardiac cycle is one complete heartbeat. It has two main phases: diastole, when the heart chambers relax and fill with blood, and systole, when the chambers contract and pump blood out. A full cycle lasts about 0.8 seconds at a resting heart rate of 75 beats per minute.

Why does the left side of the heart have a thicker wall?+

The left ventricle pumps blood to the entire body, which requires much higher pressure than pumping to the nearby lungs. That extra pressure demand is why its muscular wall is roughly three times thicker than the right ventricle's.

What do heart valves do?+

Heart valves act as one-way doors. The four valves — tricuspid, pulmonary, mitral and aortic — open to let blood through and snap shut to stop it flowing backward. The lub-dub sound of a heartbeat is the sound of these valves closing.

What is a normal resting heart rate?+

For most adults, a normal resting heart rate is between 60 and 100 beats per minute. Well-trained athletes often have resting rates closer to 40–60 bpm because their heart muscle is stronger and pumps more blood per beat.
