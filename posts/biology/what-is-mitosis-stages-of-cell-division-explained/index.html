Mitosis: Stages of Cell Division

One cell becomes two — identical copies, every time. Here's the step-by-step process that grows, repairs, and maintains every living thing.

8 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE DIAGRAMS INSIDE

Every time you heal a cut, grow a fingernail, or replace old blood cells, mitosis is quietly doing the work. It's one of the most fundamental processes in biology — and once you see the four stages, it's impossible to unsee.

Mitosis is the process a cell uses to divide into two genetically identical daughter cells. It's how organisms grow from a single fertilised egg into trillions of cells, how wounds close, and how tissues constantly replace worn-out cells. The process is tightly regulated, highly accurate, and happens billions of times a day inside your body right now.

What Is Mitosis?

CELL DIVISION

Mitosis is a type of cell division that produces two daughter cells with the same number of chromosomes as the original parent cell. It's the engine behind growth, tissue repair, and asexual reproduction in many organisms.

Before mitosis begins, the cell goes through interphase — a preparation phase where it grows, copies its DNA, and makes sure everything is ready. Once the DNA is duplicated, mitosis can proceed. Each chromosome now consists of two identical sister chromatids joined at a central point called the centromere. Those chromatids will eventually become the genetic material of the two new cells.

Key idea

Mitosis is about making more of the same. It creates two cells that are genetically identical to the parent. This is different from meiosis, which produces genetically unique sex cells.

The Four Stages of Mitosis, Step by Step

PROPHASE · METAPHASE · ANAPHASE · TELOPHASE

Mitosis is traditionally divided into four main stages, though in reality it's a smooth, continuous process. The stages are mainly a useful way to describe what's happening at each phase.

1. Prophase

Prophase is the opening act. Several things happen at once:

  • Chromatin (the loose, thread-like form of DNA) condenses into tightly packed, visible chromosomes — usually X-shaped because each is made of two sister chromatids.
  • The nuclear envelope, which normally surrounds the nucleus, breaks down.
  • The centrosomes (the cell's microtubule-organising centres) move to opposite poles of the cell.
  • Spindle fibers begin growing out from the centrosomes toward the centre of the cell.

At the end of prophase, the stage is set: chromosomes are visible, the nuclear barrier is gone, and the spindle machinery is in place.

2. Metaphase

Metaphase is the "middle" phase — literally. The chromosomes line up along the cell's equator, forming what's called the metaphase plate. Each chromosome is attached to spindle fibers coming from opposite poles, one fiber per sister chromatid.

This alignment is crucial: it ensures that when the chromatids are pulled apart, each daughter cell gets exactly one copy of every chromosome. The cell has a checkpoint here (the spindle checkpoint) that pauses division if any chromosome isn't properly attached.

3. Anaphase

Anaphase is the most visually dramatic stage. The spindle fibers shorten, pulling the sister chromatids apart at the centromere. Each chromatid — now an independent chromosome — is dragged toward its respective pole.

By the end of anaphase, the cell has two complete, identical sets of chromosomes, one at each end. The cell also elongates slightly as the poles push apart.

4. Telophase

Telophase is the cleanup and reset phase. Around each set of chromosomes, a new nuclear envelope forms. The chromosomes begin to decondense back into chromatin. The spindle fibers disassemble.

At this point, the cell has two nuclei within a single cytoplasm. The final physical split — cytokinesis — usually happens alongside telophase. In animal cells, a contractile ring pinches the cell into two. In plant cells, a cell plate forms down the middle, eventually becoming a new cell wall.

Remember

The order PMAT — Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase — is the standard mnemonic. Some textbooks add a "prometaphase" stage between prophase and metaphase, but the four-stage model is the most widely taught.

Mitosis vs Meiosis: What's the Difference?

Mitosis and meiosis are both forms of cell division, but they serve very different purposes. Here's a side-by-side comparison.

FeatureMitosisMeiosis
PurposeGrowth, repair, asexual reproductionProduction of gametes (sperm, egg)
Number of divisionsOneTwo
Daughter cells24
Chromosome numberSame as parent (diploid)Half of parent (haploid)
Genetic variationNone — identical copiesYes — crossing over and independent assortment
Where it happensAlmost all body cellsReproductive organs only

Why Mitosis Matters

Without mitosis, complex life as we know it wouldn't exist. Here's why it's so important:

  • Growth: A single fertilised egg becomes trillions of cells through repeated mitosis.
  • Repair: Damaged tissues — skin, muscle, bone — are rebuilt by dividing cells.
  • Replacement: Red blood cells, gut lining cells, and skin cells are constantly worn out and replaced.
  • Asexual reproduction: Many organisms (like yeast and some plants) reproduce by mitosis alone.
When mitosis goes wrong

Uncontrolled mitosis is the hallmark of cancer. When the checkpoints that normally regulate cell division fail, cells can divide endlessly, forming tumours. Understanding mitosis is therefore central to cancer research.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What is mitosis in simple words?+

Mitosis is the process a cell uses to divide into two identical daughter cells. It's how living things grow, repair damaged tissue, and replace old cells. Each new cell gets a full, exact copy of the original DNA.

What are the four stages of mitosis in order?+

The four main stages are prophase (chromosomes condense and the spindle forms), metaphase (chromosomes line up at the center), anaphase (sister chromatids pull apart to opposite ends), and telophase (two new nuclei form). Cytokinesis then splits the cell into two.

What is the difference between mitosis and meiosis?+

Mitosis produces two genetically identical diploid cells for growth and repair. Meiosis produces four genetically unique haploid cells (gametes) for sexual reproduction. Mitosis has one division round; meiosis has two.

Why is mitosis important for living things?+

Mitosis lets organisms grow from a single cell, replace worn-out or damaged cells, and heal wounds. Without it, tissues couldn't repair themselves and multicellular life couldn't develop or survive.

What happens during anaphase?+

During anaphase, the sister chromatids are pulled apart by spindle fibers and move to opposite poles of the cell. This ensures each daughter cell will receive one complete copy of every chromosome.

Does mitosis happen in all cells?+

Most body (somatic) cells undergo mitosis, but some specialized cells like mature neurons and red blood cells do not divide. Prokaryotes use a different process called binary fission.
