What Are Chemical Bonds?

Ionic, covalent, and metallic — why atoms stick together, and why it changes everything about a material.

8 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE DIAGRAMS INSIDE

Salt dissolves in water. Diamond scratches steel. Copper wire carries electricity. None of it happens without chemical bonds.

Atoms rarely exist alone. They join together — sometimes giving away electrons, sometimes sharing them, sometimes pooling them into a communal sea. These joinings are chemical bonds, and they explain why table salt forms crystals, why water is a liquid at room temperature, and why gold can be hammered into sheets thinner than a human hair.

BOND 01

Ionic

Electrons are transferred from one atom to another. Oppositely charged ions attract and form a rigid lattice.

BOND 02

Covalent

Electrons are shared between two atoms. This forms molecules and the strongest bonds in chemistry.

BOND 03

Metallic

Electrons flow freely around positive ions. This is the "electron sea" that makes metals shiny and conductive.

Why Atoms Form Bonds at All

THE DRIVE FOR STABILITY

Atoms bond for one main reason: to become more stable. Specifically, most atoms "want" a full outermost electron shell — usually eight electrons, a guideline called the octet rule. Noble gases like neon and argon already have full shells, which is why they almost never react.

Everything else has to rearrange. Some atoms lose electrons. Some gain them. Some share. The result is always a lower-energy, more stable arrangement — and that's what we call a chemical bond.

The bottom line

A bond is simply an attractive force that holds atoms together. The type of bond depends entirely on how the electrons behave: transferred, shared, or pooled.

Ionic Bonds: When Electrons Are Stolen

METAL + NONMETAL

An ionic bond forms when one atom gives away one or more electrons to another atom. The atom that loses electrons becomes a positive ion (cation); the one that gains them becomes a negative ion (anion). Opposite charges attract, and the two stick together.

Ionic bonds typically form between a metal (which easily loses electrons) and a nonmetal (which easily gains them). Sodium chloride — table salt — is the classic example: sodium gives one electron to chlorine, forming Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

Sodium (Na) gives an electron to chlorine (Cl).

Properties of ionic compounds

  • High melting points — the electrostatic attraction in the lattice is strong and takes a lot of energy to break.
  • Hard and brittle — layers can't slide because ions of the same charge would repel.
  • Conduct electricity when dissolved or molten — the ions become free to move. Solid ionic compounds do not conduct.
  • Often soluble in water — water molecules pull the lattice apart by attracting individual ions.
Real-world example

Table salt (NaCl), baking soda (NaHCO₃), and the calcium in your bones and teeth are all held together by ionic bonds. The hardness of these materials comes directly from the strength of those ionic attractions.

Covalent Bonds: When Electrons Are Shared

NONMETAL + NONMETAL

A covalent bond forms when two atoms share a pair of electrons. Instead of one atom losing and the other gaining, both atoms hold on to the electrons together. This usually happens between two nonmetals that both want to gain electrons but neither wants to lose them.

The shared electrons count toward the outer shell of both atoms, satisfying the octet rule for each — without the drama of a full transfer.

Two hydrogen atoms share their single electrons to form H₂.

Properties of covalent compounds

  • Lower melting and boiling points — individual molecules are held together by weak intermolecular forces, not strong bonds.
  • Poor electrical conductivity — no free ions or electrons to carry charge.
  • Often soft or flexible — think of wax, plastic, or sugar.
  • Diamond is the exception — a giant covalent network where every carbon atom is bonded to four others, making it one of the hardest known materials.
Real-world example

Water (H₂O), carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), and the DNA in your cells are all held together by covalent bonds. So is diamond — the hardest natural substance on Earth.

Metallic Bonds: The Electron Sea

METAL + METAL

A metallic bond forms when metal atoms pool their outermost electrons together. The positive metal ions sit in a regular lattice, while the electrons flow freely around them like a sea of negative charge.

This "electron sea" is what makes metals behave the way they do: the delocalized electrons can carry both electricity and heat, and the layers of ions can slide past each other without breaking the bond — which is why metals are malleable and ductile rather than brittle.

Positive ions are fixed; electrons drift freely between them.

Properties of metallic bonds

  • Conduct electricity and heat — the free electrons move easily through the lattice.
  • Malleable and ductile — layers of ions slide without shattering the bond.
  • Lustrous (shiny) — free electrons absorb and re-emit light at the surface.
  • Variable melting points — mercury melts at −39°C, while tungsten melts at 3,422°C. The bond strength depends on the metal.
Real-world example

Copper wiring, gold jewelry, aluminum foil, and the steel frame of a bridge all rely on metallic bonds. The free electrons are the reason these materials conduct electricity and can be shaped without cracking.

Which Bond Is Strongest?

There's no single answer, because "strength" depends on what you're measuring. Covalent bonds are generally the strongest individual bonds — breaking the bond between two carbon atoms in diamond requires enormous energy. Ionic bonds are also very strong, but they can be broken by dissolving the compound in water, where the lattice falls apart into free ions. Metallic bonds vary widely: some metals are soft and melt easily, while others like tungsten have extremely high melting points.

Where You See Chemical Bonds in Action

Chemical bonds aren't abstract — they're why materials behave the way they do, and why engineers choose one material over another.

  • Electronics: copper wires work because metallic bonds let electrons flow freely.
  • Medicine: the covalent bonds in drug molecules determine how they interact with your body.
  • Construction: steel and aluminum are chosen for their metallic bond properties — strength, conductivity, and workability.
  • Cooking: salt dissolves in water because ionic bonds give way to attractions with water molecules.
  • Jewelry: gold is malleable and doesn't tarnish because of its metallic bond structure and electron configuration.
Why it matters

Understanding bonds is understanding why matter does what it does. Once you know how electrons behave, you can predict whether a substance will conduct electricity, dissolve in water, melt at high heat, or shatter under pressure.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What are chemical bonds in simple words?+

Chemical bonds are the attractive forces that hold atoms together. They form when atoms transfer or share electrons to become more stable, usually by filling their outermost electron shell.

What is the difference between ionic and covalent bonds?+

Ionic bonds form when one atom transfers electrons to another, creating positively and negatively charged ions that attract each other. Covalent bonds form when two atoms share electrons instead of transferring them. Ionic bonds usually form between metals and nonmetals, while covalent bonds usually form between nonmetals.

What is a metallic bond?+

A metallic bond is the attraction between positively charged metal ions and a sea of delocalized electrons that move freely throughout the metal. This explains why metals conduct electricity, are malleable, and have high melting points.

Why do atoms form chemical bonds?+

Atoms form bonds to reach a more stable, lower-energy state, typically by achieving a full outer electron shell. This is known as the octet rule for most main-group elements.

Which bond is strongest: ionic, covalent, or metallic?+

It depends on the specific substance. Covalent bonds (like in diamond) are generally the strongest individual bonds. Ionic bonds are strong but can break when dissolved in water. Metallic bonds vary widely but often produce very high melting points. There is no single strongest bond type across all materials.

What is a real-world example of each bond type?+

Table salt (NaCl) is a classic ionic bond. Water (H₂O) and diamond are covalent. Copper wire and gold jewelry are metallic. Each bond type gives the material its distinctive properties.
