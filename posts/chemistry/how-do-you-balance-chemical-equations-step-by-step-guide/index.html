How Do You Balance Chemical Equations?

The step-by-step method, the rules that never change, and the tricks that make it fast — atoms in, atoms out.

8 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE BALANCER INSIDE

A chemical equation is a sentence written in symbols. Balancing it is just making sure the sentence tells the truth: no atoms appear from nowhere, and none quietly disappear.

When you write H₂ + O₂ → H₂O, you've described a real reaction — but the numbers don't add up. There are two oxygen atoms on the left and only one on the right. Balancing a chemical equation means adjusting the numbers in front of each formula until every element has the same atom count on both sides of the arrow. This guide walks through why that matters, the exact order to work in, and the shortcuts that save time once the basics click.

RULE 01

Atoms are conserved

Matter is never created or destroyed. The same atoms that go in must come out.

RULE 02

Change coefficients only

Big numbers in front of formulas may change. Small subscripts inside a formula never do.

RULE 03

Finish with whole numbers

Use the smallest possible whole-number ratio. Fractions are a stepping stone, not the answer.

Why Balancing Matters: The Law of Conservation of Mass

CONSERVATION OF MASS

In a closed system, the total mass before a chemical reaction equals the total mass after it. Atoms rearrange, bonds break and form, but no atom is turned into something else or thrown away. That single fact is the entire reason equations must balance.

If the equation H₂ + O₂ → H₂O were accurate as written, it would mean one oxygen atom vanished during the reaction — which contradicts the conservation of mass. Balancing fixes the atom count without changing which substances are involved.

A concrete way to see it

Imagine two sealed jars. One contains hydrogen and oxygen gas; the other contains water. If you could count every atom in each jar, the totals would match. A chemical equation is simply a written promise that this atom count stays constant.

Coefficients vs. Subscripts: The One Rule That Trips Everyone

Before balancing anything, it's worth being crystal clear about what you're allowed to change and what you aren't.

SymbolWhere it sitsWhat it meansCan you change it?
CoefficientIn front of a formulaHow many units of that substance take partYes — this is the balancing tool
SubscriptInside a formula, bottom rightHow many atoms of an element are in one unitNo — it defines the substance

Changing a subscript changes the substance. H₂O is water. H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide — a different compound with different properties. If you balance an equation by editing subscripts, you've silently swapped one chemical for another and the equation is no longer describing the same reaction.

Changing a coefficient, on the other hand, only changes how much of a substance is involved. Turning H₂O into 2H₂O still means water — just two units of it. That's why coefficients are the only legal move.

The Step-by-Step Method

THE WORKFLOW

Balancing gets much easier when you follow a consistent order instead of jumping between elements. Use this sequence every time.

  1. Write the unbalanced equation with correct formulas for every reactant and product. Get the formulas right before touching any numbers.
  2. Count atoms of each element on the reactant side and the product side. A quick table keeps this organized.
  3. Balance metals first, then non-metals, then hydrogen, and save oxygen for last. Elements appearing in only one compound on each side are the easiest starting points.
  4. Treat polyatomic ions as single units if they appear unchanged on both sides. Balancing SO₄ as one block is faster and less error-prone than counting sulfur and oxygen separately.
  5. Use coefficients, never subscripts to adjust the counts. Start with the smallest coefficient that fixes an imbalance, usually 2 or 3.
  6. Recount everything after each change. A coefficient affects every atom in that formula, so a single edit can ripple across several elements.
  7. Clear any fractions by multiplying the entire equation by the denominator, then confirm every coefficient is a whole number in the smallest possible ratio.

Worked example: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

  1. Count atoms. Left: 2 hydrogen, 2 oxygen. Right: 2 hydrogen, 1 oxygen.
  2. Oxygen is unbalanced and appears in only one compound on each side, so start there. Put a 2 in front of H₂O: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
  3. Recount. Left: 2 hydrogen, 2 oxygen. Right: 4 hydrogen, 2 oxygen.
  4. Hydrogen is now short on the left. Put a 2 in front of H₂: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
  5. Final count. Left: 4 hydrogen, 2 oxygen. Right: 4 hydrogen, 2 oxygen. Balanced.

Trick 1: Treat Polyatomic Ions as One Unit

Many equations contain groups like sulfate SO₄, nitrate NO₃, phosphate PO₄, or ammonium NH₄ that stay intact on both sides of the arrow. When that happens, balance the group as a single block instead of splitting it into individual atoms.

Example: AgNO₃ + CaCl₂ → AgCl + Ca(NO₃)₂

  1. Notice NO₃ appears on both sides as a complete group. Count it as one unit: 1 nitrate on the left, 2 on the right.
  2. Balance nitrate by placing a 2 in front of AgNO₃: 2AgNO₃ + CaCl₂ → AgCl + Ca(NO₃)₂.
  3. Now silver is off. Put a 2 in front of AgCl: 2AgNO₃ + CaCl₂ → 2AgCl + Ca(NO₃)₂.
  4. Recount everything. Calcium: 1 left, 1 right. Chlorine: 2 left, 2 right. Silver: 2 left, 2 right. Nitrate: 2 left, 2 right. Balanced.
Why this works

The polyatomic ion behaves as a single particle during the reaction. It may swap partners, but it doesn't break apart — so the math is identical whether you count it as a group or as its individual atoms. Counting it as a group is just faster and less likely to produce an arithmetic slip.

Trick 2: Balance Combustion Reactions in the Right Order

Combustion of a hydrocarbon in oxygen follows a predictable pattern: carbon and hydrogen come from the fuel, and oxygen is the wild card because it appears in multiple products. Balance in this order:

  1. Carbon first. Every carbon in the fuel becomes one CO₂, so set the CO₂ coefficient equal to the number of carbon atoms in the fuel.
  2. Hydrogen second. Every two hydrogen atoms in the fuel become one H₂O, so divide the hydrogen count by two to get the water coefficient.
  3. Oxygen last. Total the oxygen atoms on the product side, then divide by two to find the O₂ coefficient on the reactant side.
  4. Clear fractions. If the oxygen coefficient comes out as a fraction, multiply every coefficient in the equation by two.

Worked example: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

  1. Carbon: 1 in CH₄, so CO₂ gets a coefficient of 1. Equation so far: CH₄ + O₂ → 1CO₂ + H₂O.
  2. Hydrogen: 4 in CH₄, so water gets a coefficient of 2. Equation: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
  3. Oxygen on the product side: 2 from CO₂ plus 2 from 2H₂O totals 4. The reactant side therefore needs 4 oxygen atoms, which means an O₂ coefficient of 2.
  4. Final equation: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Recount: 1 carbon, 4 hydrogen, 4 oxygen on each side.

Worked example with a fraction: C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

  1. Carbon: 2 in C₂H₆, so CO₂ gets a coefficient of 2.
  2. Hydrogen: 6 in C₂H₆, so water gets a coefficient of 3.
  3. Oxygen on the product side: 4 from 2CO₂ plus 3 from 3H₂O totals 7. An O₂ coefficient of 3.5 would supply 7 oxygen atoms, but coefficients must be whole numbers.
  4. Multiply every coefficient by 2: 2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O. Recount and confirm: 4 carbon, 12 hydrogen, 14 oxygen on each side.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

  • Editing subscripts. This changes the substance. If you find yourself wanting to change a subscript, the equation likely needs a different coefficient somewhere else.
  • Forgetting that coefficients multiply everything. A coefficient of 3 in front of Ca(NO₃)₂ means 3 calcium, 6 nitrogen, and 18 oxygen — not 3 of each.
  • Leaving fractions in the final answer. Fractions are fine as a working step but must be cleared before you're done.
  • Stopping at the first balanced version. Always check whether the coefficients share a common factor. If they do, divide them down to the smallest whole-number ratio.
  • Balancing oxygen first. Oxygen often appears in several compounds on both sides, making it the hardest place to start. Save it for last.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

Why do you balance chemical equations?+

You balance a chemical equation because matter is never created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. The number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides of the arrow, which is the law of conservation of mass.

What is the difference between a coefficient and a subscript?+

A subscript is a small number written inside a chemical formula that tells you how many atoms of an element are in one unit of that substance. A coefficient is a big number written in front of a formula that multiplies every atom in that formula. You may change coefficients when balancing, but never subscripts.

Can you ever change a subscript to balance an equation?+

No. Changing a subscript changes the identity of the substance itself. H₂O is water, but H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide — a completely different compound. Only coefficients may be adjusted when balancing.

What is the fastest order to balance a chemical equation?+

Balance elements in this order: metals first, then non-metals, then hydrogen, then oxygen last. Leave hydrogen and oxygen until the end because they often appear in multiple compounds, and treat polyatomic ions that stay together on both sides as single units.

How do you balance a combustion reaction?+

For a hydrocarbon burning in oxygen, balance carbon first, then hydrogen, then oxygen last. If the oxygen count comes out as a fraction, multiply every coefficient in the entire equation by two to get whole numbers.

How do you know when a chemical equation is fully balanced?+

An equation is balanced when a final tally shows the same number of atoms of every element on the reactant side and the product side, and every coefficient is the smallest possible whole number.
