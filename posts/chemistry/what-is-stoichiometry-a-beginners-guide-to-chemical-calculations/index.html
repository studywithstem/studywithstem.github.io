Stoichiometry, minus the confusion

Mole ratios, limiting reactants, and the maths behind every balanced equation — explained with real examples and a live calculator.

9 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE CALCULATOR INSIDE

If a recipe calls for 2 eggs per 1 cup of flour, you'd never use 5 eggs with 1 cup of flour and expect a normal cake. Stoichiometry is exactly that — but for chemical reactions. It tells you how much of each substance you need and how much product you'll get.

Every balanced chemical equation is a recipe written in moles. Stoichiometry is the set of rules that lets you use those mole ratios to calculate masses, volumes, or concentrations of reactants and products. It's the single most practical skill in introductory chemistry — and once you see the pattern, it never really changes.

What Stoichiometry Actually Means

THE BIG PICTURE

The word comes from the Greek stoicheion (element) and metron (measure). In practice, it's the bridge between the microscopic world of atoms and the macroscopic world of grams and litres.

Here's the key idea: a balanced chemical equation tells you the ratio in which particles react. For example:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O 2 molecules of hydrogen react with 1 molecule of oxygen to form 2 molecules of water.

That same ratio applies to moles: 2 moles of H₂ react with 1 mole of O₂ to produce 2 moles of H₂O. And because moles convert to grams using molar mass, the ratio also tells you the mass relationship.

The one rule that never changes

Before you do any stoichiometry calculation, the equation must be balanced. An unbalanced equation gives you the wrong mole ratio, which makes every calculation wrong from that point forward.

Mole Ratios: The Engine of Stoichiometry

USING COEFFICIENTS

A mole ratio is simply a fraction built from the coefficients of two substances in a balanced equation. Once you have the balanced equation, you can write a mole ratio between any two substances in it.

For the reaction N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃:

  • Mole ratio of N₂ to H₂ is 1:3 → 1 mol N₂ / 3 mol H₂ or 3 mol H₂ / 1 mol N₂
  • Mole ratio of H₂ to NH₃ is 3:2 → 3 mol H₂ / 2 mol NH₃
  • Mole ratio of N₂ to NH₃ is 1:2 → 1 mol N₂ / 2 mol NH₃

You choose which ratio to use based on what you're given and what you need to find. The substance you know goes on the bottom; the substance you want goes on top.

Quick check: reading a balanced equation

Consider the combustion of methane:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O 1 mole of CH₄ reacts with 2 moles of O₂ to produce 1 mole of CO₂ and 2 moles of H₂O.

If you have 3 moles of CH₄, the ratio tells you immediately that you need 6 moles of O₂, and you'll produce 3 moles of CO₂ and 6 moles of H₂O. No unit conversion needed yet — that's the beauty of working in moles.

Limiting Reactant: Who Runs Out First?

THE BOTTLENECK

In the real world, reactants aren't always present in the exact ratio the equation demands. When one reactant runs out before the others, the reaction stops — no matter how much of the other reactants remain.

The reactant that runs out first is the limiting reactant. It controls the maximum amount of product that can form. The others are in excess.

How to find the limiting reactant — every time

  1. Balance the chemical equation.
  2. Convert the amount of each reactant to moles.
  3. Divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient in the balanced equation.
  4. The smallest result is the limiting reactant.
  5. Use the limiting reactant's moles to calculate product amounts.
Why dividing by the coefficient matters

You can't just compare moles directly. A reactant with a larger coefficient needs proportionally more moles to react. Dividing by the coefficient normalises everything to the same scale, so the comparison is fair.

Worked example

Consider the reaction 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. You have 4 moles of H₂ and 3 moles of O₂. Which is limiting?

  1. Balanced equation: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
  2. Moles of H₂ = 4; moles of O₂ = 3.
  3. Divide by coefficients: H₂ → 4 ÷ 2 = 2. O₂ → 3 ÷ 1 = 3.
  4. Smallest is 2, so H₂ is the limiting reactant.
  5. O₂ is in excess. Product formed depends on H₂: 4 moles H₂ × (2 mol H₂O / 2 mol H₂) = 4 moles H₂O.

Percent Yield: What You Get vs What You Expected

REALITY CHECK

Stoichiometry gives you the theoretical yield — the maximum product possible if everything reacted perfectly. In a real lab or factory, you almost never get that much. Side reactions, incomplete reactions, and losses during purification all reduce the amount you actually collect.

% Yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100 Actual yield = what you measured. Theoretical yield = what stoichiometry predicts.

A percent yield above 100% usually means the product isn't pure — there's solvent or another impurity adding mass. A yield below 100% is normal and expected.

Worked example

Stoichiometry predicts 20 g of product, but you isolate only 15 g. What's the percent yield?

  1. Formula: % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100
  2. Substitute: (15 g ÷ 20 g) × 100
  3. Solve: 75%

Where Stoichiometry Shows Up in Real Life

  • Medicine: calculating exact drug doses based on reaction yields and purity.
  • Manufacturing: determining how much raw material to buy so nothing runs out mid-production.
  • Environmental science: predicting how much CO₂ a given amount of fuel will release.
  • Food science: scaling recipes in industrial food production while keeping chemical ratios correct.
  • Energy: calculating fuel-to-energy ratios for combustion engines and power plants.
The pattern behind every stoichiometry problem

Convert what you're given to moles → use the mole ratio from the balanced equation → convert moles to whatever unit the question asks for. That three-step rhythm handles almost every stoichiometry problem you'll meet in a first-year chemistry course.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What is stoichiometry in simple words?+

Stoichiometry is the maths of chemical reactions. It uses the balanced equation to figure out exactly how much of each substance reacts and how much product forms, using mole ratios.

What is a mole ratio?+

A mole ratio is the ratio of coefficients from a balanced chemical equation. For example, in 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the mole ratio of H₂ to O₂ is 2:1, and H₂ to H₂O is 2:2 (or 1:1).

How do you find the limiting reactant?+

Convert each reactant's amount to moles, divide by its coefficient from the balanced equation, and compare. The smallest result is the limiting reactant — it runs out first and controls how much product forms.

What is percent yield?+

% yield = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100. It measures how efficient a reaction was, with 100% being the maximum possible.

Why do we need a balanced equation for stoichiometry?+

The coefficients in a balanced equation give the mole ratios that link reactants to products. Without balancing, the ratios are wrong and all calculations will be off.

What is the difference between theoretical and actual yield?+

Theoretical yield is the maximum product predicted by stoichiometry, assuming perfect conditions. Actual yield is what you really get in the lab — always less due to side reactions, incomplete reactions, or losses during purification.
