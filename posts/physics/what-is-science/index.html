What is Science?

A complete beginner's guide to the definition of science, the method behind every discovery ever made, and the difference between a hypothesis, a law and a theory — explained without the jargon.

Ask ten people what is science and you'll collect ten different answers — test tubes and lab coats, chalkboards full of equations, "the hard classes at school." Strip away the costumes and you're left with something far more interesting: science is the most reliable system humans have ever built for figuring out what is actually true about the world.

This guide takes the question apart properly — the definition, the method behind every discovery ever made, the hallmarks that separate real science from lookalikes, and the branches you can explore next. No lab coat required.

The Definition of Science

The word science comes from the Latin scientia, meaning knowledge. But the modern definition is sharper than "knowing things" — it's knowledge earned in a very particular way:

"Science is the pursuit and application of knowledge and understanding of the natural and social world following a systematic methodology based on evidence." — THE SCIENCE COUNCIL, UK

Two phrases in that sentence do all the heavy lifting:

  • A systematic methodology — science is a process: a disciplined loop of observing, questioning, predicting, testing and checking that anyone can follow and repeat.
  • Based on evidence — in science, the final judge is never authority, tradition, confidence or votes. It's what careful measurement of the world actually shows.

That's why science is really two things at once: a body of knowledge — facts like "water is H₂O," laws like gravitation — and the method that keeps producing, testing and correcting that knowledge. Remove the method and you're left with a pile of claims; remove the knowledge and you have an empty recipe. Science needs both.

It's equally worth saying what science is not. It isn't a belief system, it isn't "whatever scientists say" (scientists are human, and the method exists precisely because humans are fallible), and it isn't the same thing as technology — though the two feed each other constantly.

The Scientific Method, Step by Step

Every scientific discovery — from why apples fall to why vaccines work — travelled the same road. The classic version has eight steps:

  1. Observation — notice something in nature and describe it carefully.
  2. Question — turn the observation into a specific, answerable question.
  3. Hypothesis — propose a testable explanation: an educated guess that could be wrong.
  4. Prediction — derive a consequence: if the hypothesis is true, then you should observe X.
  5. Experiment — design a controlled test that checks the prediction — and could refute it.
  6. Analysis — compare the data with the prediction using statistics and measurement.
  7. Peer review and replication — other scientists critique the work and repeat it independently.
  8. Conclusion — the hypothesis is supported, refined or rejected, and the loop starts again.

Here's the loop in action with a real question: why does the sky glow red at sunset? The observation: sunlight looks blue overhead all day but turns red near the horizon. The hypothesis: air molecules scatter short blue wavelengths far more strongly than long red ones. The prediction: when sunlight travels through much more atmosphere — say, after a volcano loads the air with dust — sunsets should turn spectacularly red. The test: after the 1883 eruption of Krakatoa, observers worldwide recorded blood-red sunsets and blue-green moons, exactly as predicted. The explanation then survived every further test thrown at it for over a century — which is how it earned its name: Rayleigh scattering.

KEY INSIGHT

A hypothesis never becomes "true" — it becomes not yet false. Every new experiment is another chance to break it, and an explanation that has survived thousands of those chances is what science calls a theory.

The Six Hallmarks of Science

Strip the process down to its rules and you get six signatures that show up in every genuine science — and whose absence is the fastest way to spot a fake:

H/01

Empirical

Claims come from what can be measured, seen or detected — not from intuition, authority or revelation.

H/02

Testable

Every assertion must suggest an experiment that could confirm it — or contradict it.

H/03

Falsifiable

As philosopher Karl Popper insisted: if nothing could ever count against an idea, it isn't science.

H/04

Reproducible

Independent teams — other labs, other countries, other decades — get the same answer.

H/05

Objective

Controls, blind trials and statistics exist to stop human beliefs from shaping the results.

H/06

Self-correcting

Older ideas get revised or replaced when better evidence arrives. No exceptions, no heroes.

Hypothesis vs. Law vs. Theory

Three words cause more confusion in science than any others, so let's fix them once and for all. A hypothesis is a testable guess — the starting point. A law describes what happens, usually as a compact equation. A theory explains why it happens, and is the strongest status an explanation can reach: backed by so much independent evidence that rejecting it would mean rejecting mountains of data.

StatusWhat it isExample
HypothesisA single, testable, provisional explanation — right or wrong, it hasn't faced the data yet."Bread mould grows faster in humid air."
LawA concise — often mathematical — description of a pattern, valid everywhere it has been tested.Newton's law of universal gravitation.
TheoryA well-substantiated explanation of why, confirmed across many independent lines of evidence.Germ theory, evolution, general relativity.

Note what's missing from that ladder: theories don't "graduate" into laws. They're different jobs, not different ranks. Newton's law of gravitation tells you what masses do; Einstein's general theory of relativity explains why spacetime makes them do it.

The Branches of Science

"Science" is an umbrella over a whole family of disciplines, usually grouped into three tiers: the natural sciences that study the physical world, the formal sciences that supply the language, and the social and applied sciences that extend the method to people and technology.

NAT.01

Physics

Matter, energy, motion and the fundamental forces — from quarks to galaxies. (This post lives here.)

NAT.02

Chemistry

What matter is made of and how it transforms — atoms, molecules and every reaction between them.

NAT.03

Biology

Life itself: cells, genetics, evolution and ecosystems, from bacteria to whales.

FOR.01

Mathematics & Logic

The formal sciences — the abstract toolkit every other branch depends on to stay consistent.

SOC.01

Social Sciences

The scientific method pointed at humans: psychology, economics, sociology, archaeology.

APP.01

Applied & Engineering

Science turned into tools: medicine, robotics, computing, aerospace — where STEM earns its E.

Science vs. Pseudoscience

Plenty of claims borrow the look of science — the jargon, the white coats, the charts — without the substance. Karl Popper's demarcation line is simple: a claim is scientific only if it could, in principle, be proven wrong.

The fastest red flags:

  • The claim can't be tested or falsified — it "explains" every outcome after the fact.
  • Results are never submitted to peer review, or appear only in fringe "special journals."
  • Evidence is cherry-picked, and contradicting studies are ignored.
  • It leans on secrets — "they don't want you to know" replaces replication.
  • Critics are attacked personally instead of the data being re-tested.

Real science, by contrast, publishes its methods precisely so its enemies can destroy its ideas. If nobody has managed to yet, that isn't weakness — that's the strongest position in human knowledge.

Why Science Matters (Even If You Never Enter a Lab)

You don't have to be a scientist to live inside its results — you already do. Every morning you wake up inside an experiment that worked:

  • Medicine — vaccines, antibiotics and anaesthesia came from the method, not from optimism.
  • GPS — relativity makes satellite clocks run about \( 38\,\mu\text{s/day} \) fast; uncorrected, your map position would drift roughly 10 km every single day. Einstein literally keeps you on the road.
  • Weather forecasts — physics run on supercomputers now predicts a hurricane's track days in advance.
  • Smartphones — transistors and lasers are quantum mechanics engineered into a pocket-sized slab.
  • Clean water and safe food — microbiology and chemistry quietly save more lives than any other application of science.

And beyond the gadgets, the scientific mindset is a life skill: demand evidence, hold opinions provisionally, update when the facts change. It's the closest thing to a cheat code for not being fooled.

How to Think Like a Scientist

You can run the method on everyday questions without a single beaker. Five habits get you most of the way:

  1. Ask "how do you know?" — of headlines, of ads, and especially of yourself.
  2. Try to break your own ideas — the fastest way to find out if they're real.
  3. Prefer measurement over memory — write the numbers down; brains quietly edit memories.
  4. Change your mind in public — updating on new evidence is a promotion, not an embarrassment.
  5. Stay curious longer than you stay confident.

Key Takeaways

SUMMARY — SKIM THIS IN 30 SECONDS
  • Science is a method (observe → question → hypothesize → predict → test → review) and the knowledge that method produces.
  • Testability and falsifiability are the boundary between science and non-science.
  • Hypothesis = testable guess · Law = what happens · Theory = why it happens. One never "graduates" into another.
  • Branches: natural (physics, chemistry, biology), formal (math, logic), social and applied.
  • Science is provisional and self-correcting — and that is exactly its superpower.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is science in simple words?

Science is the organized way of asking questions about the natural world and answering them with evidence: observe something, ask why, propose a testable answer, run experiments, and keep the answer only if the data supports it.

What are the main branches of science?

The natural sciences study the physical world and include physics, chemistry and biology. The formal sciences (mathematics, logic, statistics) provide the language, while the social and applied sciences (psychology, economics, engineering, medicine, robotics) extend the same method to people and technology.

Is a scientific theory just a guess?

No. In science the guess stage is called a hypothesis. A theory is the strongest status an explanation can reach: an idea, like evolution or germ theory, that has survived decades of independent testing and explains a huge range of evidence.

What is the difference between a scientific law and a theory?

A law describes what happens, usually as an equation, such as Newton's law of gravitation. A theory explains why it happens, as Einstein's general relativity explains gravity. Laws and theories are different jobs, not ranks — one does not become the other.

Can science prove anything with 100% certainty?

No. Science builds confidence through repeated, independent testing rather than absolute proof, and it always stays open to new evidence. That provisional, self-correcting stance is what makes it so reliable.

Who invented the scientific method?

No single person. Ibn al-Haytham's optics experiments around 1000 CE, Galileo's controlled tests in the 1600s and Francis Bacon's written framework all shaped the modern step-by-step process.
