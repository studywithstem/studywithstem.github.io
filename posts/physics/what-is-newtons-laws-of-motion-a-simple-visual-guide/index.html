Newton's Laws of Motion

Why things keep moving, why pushes matter, and why every force has a partner. Three rules, one visual guide.

7 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE DIAGRAMS INSIDE

Drop a ball, push a cart, jump off a boat — and you've already lived through all three of Newton's laws before breakfast. Here's what's actually happening, in plain language, with the diagrams to prove it.

In 1687, Isaac Newton published three simple statements that still describe how nearly everything in daily life moves — cars, planets, footballs, rockets. They're called Newton's laws of motion, and together they explain why objects start moving, stop moving, speed up, slow down, or push back.

LAW 01

Inertia

An object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion, unless a force acts on it.

LAW 02

F = ma

Force equals mass times acceleration — bigger forces and lighter objects accelerate faster.

LAW 03

Action-Reaction

For every force one object applies, a second object pushes back with equal force in the opposite direction.

Newton's First Law: The Law of Inertia

LAW OF INERTIA

Newton's first law says that an object will keep doing whatever it's already doing — staying still, or moving in a straight line at a constant speed — until something pushes or pulls on it. This built-in resistance to changing motion is called inertia, and every object with mass has it.

This is why a hockey puck can glide across smooth ice for a long distance: friction is low, so almost nothing is fighting to slow it down. On a rough carpet, friction is the outside force doing the slowing.

Everyday inertia

  • A passenger lurches forward when a bus brakes suddenly — their body was still moving, the bus wasn't.
  • Shaking a ketchup bottle downward gets the sauce moving; stopping your hand suddenly lets the ketchup's inertia carry it out.
  • A tablecloth can be yanked out from under dishes if it's pulled fast enough — the dishes' inertia keeps them in place.
  • Seatbelts exist specifically to apply the outside force a body needs during a crash, since the body itself would otherwise keep moving forward.
Worth remembering

More mass means more inertia. A loaded shopping cart takes more effort to start moving and more effort to stop than an empty one — same cart, same wheels, just more resistance to a change in motion.

Newton's Second Law: F = ma

FORCE, MASS & ACCELERATION

The second law puts a number on how forces change motion. It states that the acceleration of an object depends on the net force acting on it and the object's mass.

F = m · a F = net force (newtons, N)  ·  m = mass (kilograms, kg)  ·  a = acceleration (m/s²)

Rearranged, this also tells you how to find acceleration directly:

a = F / m Double the force on the same mass, and acceleration doubles. Double the mass with the same force, and acceleration is cut in half.

Worked example

A 5 kg cart is pushed with a net force of 20 N. What's its acceleration?

  1. Start from the formula: a = F / m
  2. Substitute the values: a = 20 N ÷ 5 kg
  3. Solve: a = 4 m/s²

Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction

EVERY FORCE HAS A PAIR

The third law states that forces always come in pairs: when one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object exerts an equal force back on the first, in the opposite direction. These are usually called action-reaction pairs.

A crucial detail people often miss: the two forces in a pair act on different objects, so they never cancel each other out. That's why the reaction force doesn't stop the action from happening.

Everyday action-reaction pairs

  • A swimmer's hands push water backward; the water pushes the swimmer forward.
  • A rocket pushes exhaust gas downward at high speed; the gas pushes the rocket upward.
  • Walking works because your foot pushes the ground backward, and the ground pushes your foot forward.
  • A skateboarder who jumps off a stationary skateboard sends the board rolling one way while they move the other.

Where You'll See These Laws in Action

Newton's laws aren't confined to textbooks — engineers, athletes and drivers rely on them constantly, whether or not they ever write out the formula.

  • Car safety: seatbelts and airbags exist to supply the stopping force a body needs, since the first law says it won't stop on its own.
  • Sports: a harder kick applies more force to the same ball, which the second law predicts will accelerate it faster.
  • Rocketry: every rocket launch is a real-time demonstration of the third law, trading exhaust momentum for lift-off thrust.
  • Vehicle design: heavier vehicles need proportionally more braking force to decelerate at the same rate as lighter ones.
Where the model stops applying

Newton's laws are extremely accurate for everyday speeds and sizes — cars, balls, planets, rockets. They start breaking down only near the speed of light, where relativity takes over, and at atomic scales, where quantum mechanics takes over.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What are Newton's three laws of motion in simple words?+

The first law says an object keeps doing whatever it's already doing unless a force acts on it. The second law says force equals mass times acceleration, so bigger pushes or lighter objects speed up faster. The third law says every force has an equal and opposite reaction force acting back.

What is the formula for Newton's second law?+

Newton's second law is written as F = ma, where F is the net force in newtons, m is the mass in kilograms, and a is the acceleration in meters per second squared. Rearranged, acceleration equals net force divided by mass.

Why doesn't a pair of action-reaction forces cancel out and stop all motion?+

Action-reaction pairs always act on two different objects, never on the same one, so they can't cancel each other out. A book pushes down on a table and the table pushes up on the book — those two forces act on different objects and each still affects its own object normally.

What is inertia and how is it related to the first law?+

Inertia is an object's built-in resistance to changing its motion, and it's exactly what the first law describes. The more mass an object has, the more inertia it has, which is why a loaded shopping cart is harder to start moving or stop than an empty one.

Do Newton's laws still work at very high speeds or very small sizes?+

Newton's laws describe everyday motion extremely well, but they break down close to the speed of light, where relativity takes over, and at the scale of atoms, where quantum mechanics takes over. For cars, balls, rockets and almost everything people build or observe directly, Newton's laws remain the standard, accurate model.

What is a real-world example of each of Newton's laws?+

A passenger lurching forward when a bus stops suddenly demonstrates the first law. A shopping cart accelerating faster when pushed harder or when it's lighter demonstrates the second law. A swimmer pushing water backward to glide forward demonstrates the third law.
