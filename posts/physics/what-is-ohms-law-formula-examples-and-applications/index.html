Ohm's Law

Voltage, current and resistance — the simple equation behind every circuit you've ever used.

7 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE DIAGRAM INSIDE

Every time you flip a light switch, charge a phone, or dim a lamp, Ohm's Law is quietly doing the math. Here's what it actually says — and why it matters.

In 1827, German physicist Georg Simon Ohm published the relationship between voltage, current and resistance. It became known as Ohm's Law, and it remains the single most useful equation in electronics — the foundation for designing circuits, choosing components, and understanding what happens inside anything that plugs in.

V

Voltage

The electrical push that drives charge around a circuit. Measured in volts (V).

I

Current

The rate at which charge flows through a component. Measured in amperes (A).

R

Resistance

How strongly a component opposes the flow of current. Measured in ohms (Ω).

What Is Ohm's Law?

V = I × R

Ohm's Law states that the current through a conductor between two points is directly proportional to the voltage across those two points, and inversely proportional to the resistance between them. In plain words: push harder and more flows; make the path harder and less flows.

The law holds for ohmic conductors — metallic wires and resistors at constant temperature — where resistance stays fixed. Components like diodes and filament lamps have resistance that changes with voltage or temperature, so they don't follow a straight-line rule.

V = I · R V = voltage (volts, V)  ·  I = current (amperes, A)  ·  R = resistance (ohms, Ω)

Rearranged, the same relationship gives current from voltage and resistance, or resistance from voltage and current:

I = V / R Current increases when voltage rises or resistance falls.
R = V / I Resistance is the voltage needed to drive one ampere through a component.

Voltage, Current & Resistance Explained

These three quantities are easy to mix up. The cleanest way to tell them apart is to think about what each one actually measures.

  • Voltage (V) — the energy transferred per unit charge, or the "electrical pressure" driving the current. A 9 V battery provides more push than a 1.5 V cell.
  • Current (I) — the number of coulombs of charge passing a point per second. It's the flow rate, not the energy.
  • Resistance (R) — a measure of how much a component restricts current for a given voltage. High resistance means less current for the same push.
The water analogy

Imagine water flowing through a pipe: voltage is the pump pressure, current is the flow rate, and resistance is the narrowness of the pipe. Squeeze the pipe and less water flows for the same pressure — that's increasing resistance.

The V-I Graph

Plot voltage on the vertical axis against current on the horizontal axis. For a component obeying Ohm's Law, the result is a straight line through the origin — the signature of proportionality.

The gradient of that line equals the resistance. A steeper line means a higher resistance: more volts are needed for each ampere. A shallower line means a lower resistance and a bigger current for the same voltage.

Non-ohmic components

A filament lamp's resistance rises as it heats up, so its V-I graph curves. A diode conducts in only one direction and has a sharp turn-on voltage. These components still follow physics — they just don't obey Ohm's Law because their resistance isn't constant.

Ohm's Law Worked Examples

These three examples cover the standard ways Ohm's Law shows up in problems and in real circuit design.

Example 1 — finding current

A 12 V battery is connected across a 4 Ω resistor. What current flows?

  1. Start from the rearranged formula: I = V / R
  2. Substitute the values: I = 12 V ÷ 4 Ω
  3. Solve: I = 3 A

Example 2 — finding resistance

A current of 0.5 A flows when 6 V is applied across a component. What is its resistance?

  1. Start from the rearranged formula: R = V / I
  2. Substitute the values: R = 6 V ÷ 0.5 A
  3. Solve: R = 12 Ω

Example 3 — finding voltage

A 2 A current flows through a 15 Ω resistor. What voltage is across it?

  1. Start from the base formula: V = I × R
  2. Substitute the values: V = 2 A × 15 Ω
  3. Solve: V = 30 V
Choosing a resistor for an LED

An LED rated at 2 V and 20 mA needs to run from a 9 V supply. The resistor must drop 7 V at 0.02 A, so R = 7 ÷ 0.02 = 350 Ω. This is Ohm's Law doing real design work.

Ohm's Law in Series & Parallel Circuits

Ohm's Law applies to individual components and to whole circuits — but the rules for combining resistances depend on how components are wired.

  • Series: resistances add directly. The same current flows through every component, and the supply voltage is divided across them.
  • Parallel: the total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance. The voltage is the same across every branch, and the current splits between them.

Real-World Applications of Ohm's Law

Ohm's Law isn't just a classroom formula — it's the basis for how electrical systems are designed, protected and controlled.

  • Fuses and circuit breakers: sized by the current they must carry, calculated from the supply voltage and circuit resistance.
  • Household wiring: cable thickness is chosen so resistance stays low enough to deliver current without dangerous heating.
  • Dimmer switches: vary resistance to change current, which changes the brightness of a lamp.
  • Electric heaters: a high-resistance element draws a controlled current, converting electrical energy into heat.
  • Battery selection: matching a battery's internal resistance to a device avoids excessive voltage drop under load.
  • Sensors and strain gauges: tiny resistance changes are read as voltage changes using Ohm's Law.
Where the law has limits

Ohm's Law is an excellent approximation for most conductors at constant temperature, but it is not a fundamental law of nature. Semiconductors, superconductors and plasma all behave differently — yet even there, the relationship between voltage, current and resistance remains a starting point for analysis.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What is Ohm's Law in simple words?+

Ohm's Law says that the voltage across a conductor equals the current flowing through it multiplied by its resistance. Written as V = I × R, it links three quantities: how hard the electricity is pushed, how much is flowing, and how much the material fights the flow.

What is the formula for Ohm's Law?+

Ohm's Law is written as V = I × R, where V is voltage in volts, I is current in amperes and R is resistance in ohms. Rearranged, I = V / R gives current from voltage and resistance, and R = V / I gives resistance from voltage and current.

What is the difference between voltage, current and resistance?+

Voltage is the electrical push that drives charge around a circuit, measured in volts. Current is the rate at which charge flows, measured in amperes. Resistance is how strongly a component opposes that flow, measured in ohms. Voltage is the cause, current is the effect, and resistance is what limits it.

Why is the V-I graph a straight line for an ohmic conductor?+

For an ohmic conductor, resistance stays constant, so doubling the voltage doubles the current. Plotting voltage against current gives a straight line through the origin, and the gradient of that line equals the resistance.

Does Ohm's Law apply to all materials?+

No. Ohm's Law holds well for metallic conductors at constant temperature, but not for non-ohmic components such as filament lamps, diodes and thermistors, where resistance changes with temperature or voltage. Those components have curved V-I graphs.

What is a real-world example of Ohm's Law?+

Choosing a resistor to protect an LED is a classic example. If a 9 V battery powers an LED that needs 20 mA, Ohm's Law gives R = V / I = 9 / 0.02 = 450 ohms. Fuses, household wiring, dimmer switches and electric heaters all rely on the same relationship.
