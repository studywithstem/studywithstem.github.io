How Does Gravity Work?

The invisible force that keeps your feet on the ground, the Moon in orbit, and the cosmos in motion — explained simply.

7 MIN READ · INTERACTIVE DIAGRAMS INSIDE

Drop a pen, jump off a step, watch rain fall — gravity is so familiar we rarely stop to ask what it actually is. Here's the plain-language answer, from Newton's apples to Einstein's curves.

Gravity is the universal attraction between anything that has mass. It's the reason planets orbit stars, why tides rise and fall, and why you don't drift off into space right now. But how does gravity work, exactly? The answer has two layers: the one you can calculate with (Newton) and the one that explains why (Einstein).

NEWTON

Gravity is a force

Every mass pulls on every other mass. Double the mass, double the pull; double the distance, quarter the pull.

EINSTEIN

Gravity is geometry

Mass curves spacetime. Objects follow the straightest possible path through that curved space — which looks like a force.

EVERYDAY

Gravity is weight

Your weight is just how hard gravity pulls on your mass. Change the planet, change your weight — not your mass.

Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation

F = G·m₁·m₂ / r²

In 1687, Isaac Newton proposed that the same force pulling an apple to the ground also keeps the Moon circling Earth. He called it universal gravitation — universal because it applies everywhere, to everything.

F = G · m₁ · m₂ / r² F = gravitational force (N)  ·  G = 6.674×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²  ·  m₁, m₂ = masses (kg)  ·  r = distance between centres (m)

Three things follow directly from this formula:

  • More mass, more pull. A bowling ball attracts you more than a marble — just not enough for you to notice.
  • More distance, much less pull. Gravity weakens with the square of distance. Move twice as far away, and the force drops to one-quarter.
  • It works both ways. You pull on Earth exactly as hard as Earth pulls on you. Earth just moves imperceptibly because it's so massive.
Why you don't feel the pull of buildings

A skyscraper has enormous mass, but you're standing right next to it — and you feel nothing. That's because gravity is astonishingly weak compared to the other forces. It only dominates on astronomical scales, where the masses are truly vast.

Weight vs Mass: The Distinction That Matters

People use "weight" and "mass" interchangeably, but in physics they're completely different things — and the difference explains a lot about how gravity works.

So when someone says they'd "weigh less on the Moon", they're right about weight but wrong about mass. Your mass — your actual substance — is the same everywhere. Only the gravitational pull on it changes.

Free Fall and Weightlessness

If gravity pulls everything downward, why do astronauts float? The answer surprises most people: gravity is still working in orbit. Astronauts aren't beyond Earth's pull — the International Space Station is only about 400 km up, well within gravity's reach.

What's actually happening is free fall. The station and everything in it are falling toward Earth at the same rate. Since the astronauts and the station are falling together, they appear to float relative to each other. This condition is more accurately called microgravity or weightlessness — not zero gravity.

The falling elevator thought experiment

Imagine an elevator whose cable snaps. You and the elevator fall together. If you let go of a pen, it doesn't drop to the floor — it floats beside you, because you're all falling at the same rate. That's exactly what happens in orbit, just sideways enough to keep missing the ground.

Try it below: drop different masses and see that — ignoring air resistance — they hit the ground at exactly the same time.

Fall time (from 100 m, no air) 4.5 s each

In a vacuum, mass doesn't affect fall time. Galileo reportedly demonstrated this by dropping different weights from the Leaning Tower of Pisa.

Einstein's Deeper Picture: Curved Spacetime

Newton's law works brilliantly for almost everything, but it doesn't explain how gravity reaches across empty space. Einstein's general relativity (1915) offers a deeper answer: gravity isn't a force at all. It's the shape of spacetime.

Mass and energy tell spacetime how to curve; curved spacetime tells mass how to move. A planet orbiting the Sun isn't being tugged by an invisible rope — it's following the straightest available path through space that the Sun's mass has bent.

  • Orbits explained: The Moon moves forward while falling toward Earth, tracing a curve that never quite hits.
  • Light bends too: Even massless light follows curved spacetime, which is why distant stars appear shifted during a solar eclipse.
  • Gravitational waves: Ripples in spacetime itself, predicted by Einstein and finally detected in 2015.
Two descriptions, one reality

Newton's force law and Einstein's geometry aren't competing — they're two descriptions of the same phenomenon at different levels of precision. For launching a satellite, Newton is plenty. For GPS satellites, Einstein's corrections are essential.

Why Gravity Matters for Life and the Universe

Gravity is the architect of the cosmos. Without it, matter would never have clumped into stars and planets. Earth's atmosphere would drift away, and life as we know it couldn't exist.

It's also the only force that shapes the universe on the largest scales. Electromagnetism can push and pull, but gravity builds galaxies. It is patient, universal, and inescapable — and understanding it is one of the great human achievements.

FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What is gravity in simple words?+

Gravity is the invisible force that pulls objects with mass toward each other. It's why an apple falls to the ground, why the Moon orbits Earth, and why you stay on the ground instead of floating away.

What is Newton's law of universal gravitation?+

Newton's law states that every object with mass attracts every other object with a force equal to G times the product of their masses divided by the square of the distance between them. It's written as F = G·m₁·m₂ / r².

What is the difference between mass and weight?+

Mass is the amount of matter in an object and never changes, measured in kilograms. Weight is the force gravity exerts on that mass, measured in newtons, and it changes depending on where you are — you'd weigh less on the Moon but your mass stays the same.

Why do astronauts float in space if gravity is everywhere?+

Astronauts in orbit are not outside gravity — they're in free fall, constantly falling around Earth. Because they and their spacecraft are falling at the same rate, they appear to float relative to each other, a condition called weightlessness or microgravity.

How did Einstein change our understanding of gravity?+

Einstein's general relativity describes gravity not as a force but as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy. Objects move along curved paths because space itself is warped around heavy objects, which explains orbits and even light bending around stars.

What is a real-world example of gravity?+

The Moon orbiting Earth is a perfect example: Earth's gravity pulls the Moon inward, while the Moon's sideways motion keeps it from crashing into us, resulting in a stable orbit. Rain falling, tides rising, and a ball returning to the ground after a throw are other everyday examples.
