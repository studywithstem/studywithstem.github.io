03 · THE MATH

Three formulas run the whole panel.

Every number the panel shows you comes from one of these — and the calculator prints them substituted with your own values.

The divider equation

Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)

R1 sits between the supply and the tap, R2 between the tap and ground. The output is simply R2's share of the total — which is why two equal resistors always give exactly half the input.

Loaded divider

R2∥RL = R2·RL ÷ (R2 + RL)
Vout = Vin × R2∥RL ÷ (R1 + R2∥RL)

A load sits in parallel with R2 and drags the output down. Rule of thumb: keep the load at least 10× R2 and the sag stays in the single-digit percents.

Current, power & stiffness

I = Vin ÷ (R1 + R2)
Rout = R1·R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)

The divider burns power constantly — smaller resistors give a stiffer, lower-impedance output but drink more current. Totals between 1 kΩ and 100 kΩ are the usual sweet spot.

04 · FAQ

Quick answers.

The questions that come up again and again when building dividers — answered in plain language, with the numbers to back them up.

RULES OF THUMB
  • Vout is always ≤ Vin — dividers only step down.
  • Equal resistors give exactly half the input.
  • Keep the load at least 10× R2 to limit sag.
  • Totals of 1 kΩ–100 kΩ fit most projects.
What is a voltage divider?+

Two resistors chained in series across a voltage source, with the output taken from the point between them. The pair turns the input into a smaller, predictable fraction of itself, which is why dividers show up everywhere — reading sensors with an ADC, shifting a 5 V signal into a 3.3 V input, or setting a reference.

A potentiometer is simply an adjustable divider: the wiper taps somewhere along a resistive track.

What is the voltage divider formula?+

Vout = Vin × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) — the output is R2's share of the total resistance.

Example: 9 V across R1 = 10 kΩ and R2 = 10 kΩ gives Vout = 9 × 10 ÷ 20 = 4.5 V. Swap in R2 = 20 kΩ and you get 9 × 20 ÷ 30 = 6 V. The calculator shows this formula substituted with your numbers on every result.

Which resistor is R1 and which is R2?+

R1 is the top resistor, between the supply (Vin) and the output tap; R2 is the bottom one, from the tap down to ground. The output is always measured across R2.

Getting them backwards gives the complement — if R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) should be 30% but you swap the resistors, you'll get 70% instead.

Can a voltage divider step voltage up?+

No. A passive resistor divider can only produce an output between 0 V and Vin — the fraction R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) can never exceed 1. To get more voltage than you put in you need active circuitry: a boost converter, a charge pump, or an amplifying op-amp stage.

Can I power a device from a voltage divider?+

Not reliably. As soon as the device draws current it becomes a load in parallel with R2, pulling the output down — often far down. Dividers also burn power continuously in the resistors themselves.

For power, use a regulator (linear or buck/boost); save dividers for signals feeding high-impedance inputs like ADC pins, comparator inputs and op-amp buffers.

How do I choose R1 and R2 values?+

It's a trade-off. Small resistors (say a 1 kΩ total) give a stiff output that resists loading but waste milliamps; large ones (1 MΩ total) sip current but sag easily and pick up noise. Totals between 1 kΩ and 100 kΩ suit most projects.

If a load is attached, keep it at least 10× R2, and check the power figures — the calculator shows dissipation per resistor so you can pick suitable wattage ratings.

Why is my measured output lower than the calculated one?+

Four usual suspects: the load itself (add it in the calculator's load field to see the real value), resistor tolerance — ±5% parts can each be off, shifting the ratio, while ±1% parts fix this — the supply's own output impedance adding to R1, and a high-impedance divider being loaded by the multimeter itself. The loaded mode models the first and most common cause.
