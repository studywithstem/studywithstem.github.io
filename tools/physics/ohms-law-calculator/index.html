03 · THE FORMULAS

Every equation behind the results.

Ohm’s law plus its power cousins — V is volts, I is amps, R is ohms and P is watts.

Voltage

V = I × R

Voltage in volts is current in amps times resistance in ohms — the law itself. It gives the drop across any resistance carrying a known current.

Current

I = V ÷ R

Current is voltage divided by resistance. Double the voltage and the current doubles; double the resistance and it halves.

Resistance

R = V ÷ I

Resistance is voltage divided by current — how you size a resistor to limit a current to a chosen value.

Power

P = V × I

Power in watts is volts times amps, working in both directions: a 60 W bulb on 120 V draws 0.5 A.

Power from current

P = I2 × R

Power rises with the square of current — twice the current means four times the heat. This is what sets a resistor’s wattage rating.

Power from voltage

P = V2 ÷ R

The third power form, for when you know the voltage across a resistance but not the current through it.

04 · QUESTIONS

Frequently asked questions.

Short answers about the law, the units, the LED mode and how the calculator behaves.

What is Ohm’s law?+

Ohm’s law says the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it, with resistance as the constant of proportionality: V = I × R. Voltage in volts equals current in amps times resistance in ohms. Published by Georg Ohm in 1827, it’s the backbone of circuit analysis — 9 V across a 100 Ω resistor, for instance, pushes 0.09 A (90 mA) of current.

How do I calculate voltage, current or resistance?+

Rearrange V = I × R for whatever is missing: V = I × R, I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I. In the first tab, enter the two values you know and pick what to solve for — the answer appears live with the substitution shown. Example: I = 9 V ÷ 100 Ω = 0.09 A.

What is the Ohm’s law triangle?+

A memory aid. Draw a triangle with V on top and I and R side by side underneath, then cover the quantity you want: cover V and you’re left with I × R; cover I and you see V over R; cover R and you see V over I. It’s the same three formulas arranged so they’re hard to forget.

How do I calculate electrical power?+

Start from P = V × I — watts are volts times amps. If you only know two of V, I and R, use P = I² × R or P = V² ÷ R instead. The Power tab accepts any two of V, I, R and P and computes the other two, whichever pair you have.

What units does the calculator use?+

Plain SI units: volts (V), amps (A), ohms (Ω) and watts (W) — so 20 mA is entered as 0.02 and 4.7 kΩ as 4700. Results are shown twice, as the plain value and with engineering prefixes such as mA, kΩ and MΩ, so 0.02 A also reads 20 mA.

Why does an LED need a series resistor?+

An LED fixes its own voltage (its forward voltage) and would draw destructive current if wired straight to a supply. A resistor soaks up the leftover voltage and sets the current: R = (Vs − Vf) ÷ I. The LED tab goes further — it picks the nearest standard E24 resistor value (rounded up, so the current is never above target) and recommends a wattage rating with a 2× safety margin.

What happens if I enter zero or impossible values?+

Nothing silently wrong — the calculator validates every input and explains the problem: dividing by zero (zero current while solving for resistance), power with zero voltage, or a supply below an LED’s forward voltage each get a plain-language message. Physically, zero resistance is a short circuit and zero current is an open circuit.

It is completely free and runs entirely in your browser — nothing you type is uploaded, logged or sent to a server. No account, no sign-up, no limits.
