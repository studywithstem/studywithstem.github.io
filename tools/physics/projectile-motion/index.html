03 · THE FORMULAS

The physics under the hood.

Ideal projectile motion is two simple motions stitched together: constant horizontal velocity and constant downward acceleration. Six equations do all the work.

The two motions

x(t) = v0·cosθ·t   y(t) = h0 + v0·sinθ·t − ½g·t2

Horizontal velocity never changes; vertical velocity loses g metres per second, every second. The parabola is just these two stitched together.

Time of flight

t = (v0·sinθ + √(v02·sin2θ + 2g·h0)) / g

Solves h₀ + v₀·sinθ·t − ½gt² = 0 — the moment the projectile returns to ground level.

Maximum height

H = h0 + (v0·sinθ)2 / 2g

The apex is where vertical velocity hits zero — plain kinematics in disguise.

Range on level ground

R = v02·sin(2θ) / g

Famously maximised at 45° — but only when launch and landing heights match and the air is ignored.

Impact speed

v = √(v02 + 2g·h0)

Energy conservation: launch kinetic energy plus everything the height releases, all turned back into speed.

The path itself

y = h0 + x·tanθ − g·x2 / (2v02·cos2θ)

Height as a function of distance — the parabola the graph draws, and the equation the target solver inverts.

04 · FAQ

Projectile questions, answered.

The things students ask most about throwing things through the air — each answer backed by the formulas above.

What is projectile motion?+

It's the motion of anything launched into the air that then moves only under gravity — a thrown ball, an arrow, a stone dropped off a cliff. It splits into two independent parts: a horizontal motion at constant velocity, and a vertical motion that accelerates downward at g. Stitch them together and you get the familiar parabolic arc.

How do I calculate the range of a projectile?+

On level ground, R = v₀²·sin(2θ)/g — launch speed, angle and gravity are all you need. From a raised launch point, first find the flight time t = (v₀sinθ + √(v₀²sin²θ + 2gh₀))/g, then multiply by the horizontal speed: R = v₀cosθ · t. The Trajectory mode does both instantly and draws the arc.

How do I find the maximum height?+

A projectile rises until its vertical velocity reaches zero, so the maximum height is H = h₀ + (v₀·sinθ)²/2g. Example: a 20 m/s launch at 30° from ground level peaks at (20 × 0.5)² / (2 × 9.81) ≈ 5.1 m.

What launch angle gives the maximum range?+

45° — but only when launch and landing heights are equal and air resistance is ignored. Launch from a cliff and the best angle drops below 45°, because the extra height already buys falling time, so a flatter, faster arc wins. Add real air and it drops further — most sports balls fly farthest at roughly 20–35°.

Why do two different angles hit the same target?+

Because the trajectory equation is quadratic in tanθ: for most reachable targets there are two roots — a flat, fast low arc and a slow, looping high arc — and both parabolas pass exactly through the target. Push the target to the edge of range and the two angles merge into one maximum-range shot. The Hit-a-target mode solves both.

How does air resistance change the trajectory?+

Drag continuously steals speed, so the path stops being a symmetric parabola: the descent is steeper than the climb, the range shrinks (often by 30–50% for balls), the apex drops, and the optimal launch angle moves lower. The Air-drag mode integrates the true drag force F = ½·ρ·Cd·A·v² with a 4th-order Runge–Kutta solver and overlays the result on the vacuum curve.

Does the projectile's mass matter?+

In a vacuum, no — gravity accelerates every mass equally, so a feather and a cannonball fired identically land together. With air resistance, yes: drag depends on size and shape, not mass, so light-and-large objects slow down much more. Doubling the mass halves the drag's deceleration — one reason heavy ammunition carries farther.
