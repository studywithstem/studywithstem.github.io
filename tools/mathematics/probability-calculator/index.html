03 · THE MATH

Six rules run the whole show.

Every number this tool prints comes from one of six classic probability rules. No black boxes — here they are.

Complement rule

P(A′) = 1 − P(A)

The chance the event doesn’t happen. Rolling something other than a six: 1 − 1/6 = 5/6.

Addition rule

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

The chance of either event — the overlap is subtracted so it isn’t counted twice.

Multiplication rule

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

The chance of both events happening, when they’re independent of each other.

Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B)

The chance of A once B is already known to have happened.

Binomial formula

P(X = k) = C(n,k) · pk · (1−p)n−k

Exactly k successes in n identical, independent trials — the engine behind the Repeated trials tab.

Odds ⇄ probability

P = a ÷ (a + b)

“a to b in favor” means the event happens a times out of a + b in total.

04 · FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

How do I calculate the probability of a single event?+

Divide the number of favorable outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes: P(A) = favorable ÷ total. Rolling a 4 or more on a six-sided die has 3 favorable outcomes out of 6 total, so P = 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5 = 50%. The Single event tab also gives you the complement, the odds for and against, and the “1 in N” chance.

What’s the difference between independent and mutually exclusive events?+

Independent events don’t affect each other’s chances — knowing one happened tells you nothing about the other, like two separate coin flips. Mually exclusive events can’t happen at the same time, like one die roll being both a 2 and a 3. Independent → P(A∩B) = P(A)×P(B); exclusive → P(A∩B) = 0. Fun fact: mutually exclusive events with non-zero probabilities are never independent.

How do I find the probability of both events happening?+

If the events are independent, multiply: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). If they’re not independent, you need the overlap — either enter P(A and B) directly in Custom mode, or use the conditional form P(A and B) = P(B) × P(A|B).

What does conditional probability P(A|B) mean?+

It’s the probability of A happening given that B has already happened: P(A|B) = P(A∩B) ÷ P(B). Example: if 20% of days are rainy and 10% of days are rainy and cold, then P(cold | rainy) = 10% ÷ 20% = 50%.

How does the repeated-trials calculator work?+

It uses the binomial distribution: for n independent trials where each succeeds with probability p, the chance of exactly k successes is P(X = k) = C(n,k) · pk · (1−p)n−k. The panel also computes “at most” and “at least” cumulative probabilities, plus the expected value μ = np and standard deviation σ = √(np(1−p)).

How do I convert odds to probability?+

“a to b in favor” means the event happens a times out of (a + b), so P = a ÷ (a + b). “a to b against” means it fails a times out of (a + b), so P = b ÷ (a + b). For example, 3 to 1 in favor is P = 3 ÷ 4 = 75%. The converter also outputs decimal (European) odds and the American moneyline.

Can a probability be negative or over 100%?+

No — probability always sits between 0 (impossible) and 1 (certain), or 0% to 100%. The calculator validates every input: negative counts, chances above 100%, overlaps bigger than the events themselves, and P(A) + P(B) over 100% for exclusive events each get a plain-English error instead of a wrong number.

Is this tool free, and where does my data go?+

It’s completely free and runs entirely in your browser — nothing you type is uploaded, logged, or sent to any server. No account, no sign-up, no limits.
