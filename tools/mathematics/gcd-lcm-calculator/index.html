03 · QUESTIONS

Questions, answered

The nine questions every gcd–lcm hunt eventually arrives at — answered the way the tool works: exactly.

Q·01 How do I find the greatest common divisor (GCD)?
Divide the larger number by the smaller, replace the larger with the remainder, and repeat until the remainder is zero — the last non-zero value is the answer. For 48 and 180: 180 = 3×48 + 36, then 48 = 1×36 + 12, then 36 = 3×12 + 0, so gcd(48, 180) = 12. The same quantity is often called the highest common factor (HCF). The GCD tab writes every line of this as you type.
Q·02 How do I find the least common multiple (LCM)?
Two equivalent routes. Factor each number into primes and take the highest power of every prime that appears: 48 = 2⁴·3 and 180 = 2²·3²·5, so lcm = 2⁴·3²·5 = 720. Or use the formula lcm = a·b ÷ gcd = 8,640 ÷ 12 = 720. The tool shows both routes and cross-checks them.
Q·03 What is Euclid's algorithm, and why does it work?
It is Book VII of Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BC) — the oldest algorithm still in everyday use. It works because any common divisor of a and b also divides the remainder a − qb, so gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b); the pair keeps shrinking until the remainder hits zero. In the worst case it needs only about five times the number of digits of the smaller number (Lamé's theorem, via Fibonacci).
Q·04 What is the relationship between GCD and LCM?
For two positive integers, gcd(a, b) × lcm(a, b) = a × b — always: 12 × 720 = 8,640 = 48 × 180. For three or more numbers this becomes an inequality, g × ℓ ≤ a·b·c, with equality exactly when the numbers are pairwise coprime. The BOTH tab checks this in front of you.
Q·05 How do I compute the GCD or LCM of three numbers?
Fold the operation: gcd(a, b, c) = gcd(gcd(a, b), c), and likewise for the lcm. For 12, 18 and 24: gcd(12, 18) = 6 then gcd(6, 24) = 6; lcm(12, 18) = 36 then lcm(36, 24) = 72. The third box on the calculator is optional — leave it empty for two-number work.
Q·06 What does coprime (relatively prime) mean?
Two numbers are coprime when their GCD is 1 — they share no prime factor. 8 and 9 are coprime even though neither is prime. Coprime numbers have the pleasant property that their LCM is simply their product: lcm(8, 9) = 72.
Q·07 Where are GCD and LCM actually used?
Wherever fractions live: you divide by the GCD to reduce 180/48 to 15/4, and you build an LCM denominator to add 5/12 + 7/18 = 25/36. Beyond school: gear-tooth counts, when two repeating events coincide (buses, laps, calendars), polyrhythms in music, and RSA cryptography, whose private keys are found with the extended Euclidean algorithm.
Q·08 Can I use zero, negative numbers, or decimals?
Zero: gcd(a, 0) = a, because every integer divides 0, and lcm(a, 0) = 0; the calculator accepts zeros and states the rule on screen. Negatives: gcd is defined through absolute values, so enter magnitudes — this tool asks for non-negative integers. Decimals are not valid here; clear them by scaling — 1.5 and 2.25 become 150 and 225 by multiplying by 100, and the gcd scales by the same 100.
Q·09 Is it free, and does my input leave the page?
Completely free — no sign-up, no locked features. Every division, factorization and check runs locally in your browser; nothing is recorded or transmitted, and the page keeps working offline once it has loaded.
