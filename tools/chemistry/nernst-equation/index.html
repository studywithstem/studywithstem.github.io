03 · THE FORMULAS

One equation, five ways to use it.

Every mode above is the same relationship, just solved for a different letter.

Nernst equation

E = E° − (RT/nF)·lnQ

R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), F = 96485 C/mol. Relates measured potential to the standard potential and reaction quotient.

25°C simplified form

E = E° − (0.0592/n)·log₁₀Q

Only valid at exactly 298.15 K — this calculator uses the full RT/F term so any temperature works.

Solving for E°

E° = E + (RT/nF)·lnQ

Back out the standard potential from a real, measured cell voltage.

Solving for Q

Q = exp[(E°−E)·nF/RT]

Rearranges the exponent to reveal what concentration ratio produced a given voltage.

Solving for n

n = RT·lnQ ÷ [F(E°−E)]

Useful when the number of electrons transferred in a half-reaction isn't given directly.

Concentration cell

E = (RT/nF)·ln(C₂÷C₁)

E° = 0 because both electrodes are identical — the whole voltage comes from the concentration difference.

04 · FAQ

Questions people actually ask.

The short answers — with the long ones one tap away.

What is the Nernst equation?+

It's E = E° − (RT/nF) lnQ. It relates a cell's actual voltage (E) to its standard potential (E°), the number of electrons transferred (n), the absolute temperature (T), and the reaction quotient (Q).

What is standard electrode potential (E°)?+

E° is the cell potential measured under standard conditions: 1 M concentrations, 1 atm for gases, and usually 25°C. It's a fixed reference value looked up in standard reduction potential tables.

What is the reaction quotient Q?+

Q has the same form as the equilibrium constant expression — products over reactants — but uses actual current concentrations instead of equilibrium values. Q = 1 under standard conditions, which is why E equals E° there.

Where does the 0.0592 V constant come from?+

At 25°C (298.15 K), RT/F converted from natural log to base-10 log works out to about 0.05916 V, often rounded to 0.0592 V. That gives E = E° − (0.0592/n) log₁₀Q, valid only at 25°C.

How do you calculate a concentration cell's voltage?+

A concentration cell uses identical electrodes in solutions of different concentration, so E° = 0. Its voltage is entirely E = (RT/nF) ln(C_cathode ÷ C_anode), where the more concentrated side is the cathode.

Why does temperature affect cell potential?+

Temperature sits directly in the RT/nF term, so a hotter cell amplifies the gap between E and E° for a given reaction quotient. This calculator converts Celsius or Fahrenheit to kelvin automatically before solving.

Is this tool free, and where does my data go?+

It's completely free and runs entirely in your browser — nothing you type is uploaded, logged, or sent to any server. No account, no sign-up, no limits.
