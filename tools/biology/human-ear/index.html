    04 · IN NUMBERS

    The ear, measured.

    The human ear is a masterpiece of miniaturization — these are the numbers it keeps to.

    The ear spec sheet

    20 Hz – 20 kHz · 3 OSSICLES · ~20× AMPLIFICATION

    Your ear detects frequencies from 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. The three ossicles amplify eardrum vibrations about 20 times — a mechanical advantage that lets you hear whispers.

    Your ear in numbers

    ~15,000 HAIR CELLS · 2.5 cm EAR CANAL · 35 mm COCHLEA

    The cochlea is a coiled tube just 35 mm long containing around 15,000 hair cells. The ear canal is about 2.5 cm deep and naturally resonates at 2–4 kHz, exactly the range of human speech.

    The ossicle lever

    MALLEUS → INCUS → STAPES · 1.3 : 1 LEVER RATIO

    The malleus and incus form a lever that multiplies force by about 1.3×. Combined with the eardrum-to-stapes area ratio, the middle ear amplifies pressure by roughly 20× before the signal reaches the fluid-filled cochlea.

    05 · QUESTIONS

    Things people ask.

    Everything about how the model behaves, how honest the drawing is, and what it saves (nothing).

    Still stuck? Write to us — we read everything.

    How do I use the interactive human ear model?+

    Drag anywhere on the model to rotate the ear, scroll or pinch to zoom, and drag up or down to tilt it. Tap any part — the pinna, ear canal, eardrum, ossicles, cochlea — to open its dossier. You can also switch to the animated sound path or the exploded ossicle view.

    Is this ear model to scale or scientifically exact?+

    No. It's a stylized, idealized take on the human ear — built so every part is easy to see, click and tell apart. Real anatomy is far more intricate, and the structures are packed tightly into a space smaller than a coin.

    What are the three main regions of the ear?+

    The outer ear (pinna and ear canal), the middle ear (eardrum and the three ossicles: malleus, incus, stapes), and the inner ear (cochlea and semicircular canals). Each region plays a specific role in capturing, amplifying and translating sound.

    What do the ossicles do?+

    The three tiny bones — malleus (hammer), incus (anvil) and stapes (stirrup) — form a mechanical lever system that amplifies vibrations from the eardrum by about 20 times before passing them to the fluid-filled cochlea.

    How does the cochlea turn sound into nerve signals?+

    The cochlea is a fluid-filled, snail-shaped chamber lined with thousands of hair cells. Different frequencies cause different regions of the basilar membrane to vibrate, bending the hair cells and triggering electrical impulses that travel along the auditory nerve to the brain.

    What is the difference between the helix view and the animated view?+

    The helix view lets you freely rotate and inspect every part of the ear. The animated view traces the path of a sound wave from the pinna, through the ear canal and ossicles, into the cochlea, and finally to the auditory nerve.

    What does the spin toggle do?+

    It keeps the ear slowly turning on its own so it reads as a living 3D object rather than a flat picture. Grab it at any time to steer it yourself — the spin politely waits while you're inspecting.

    Does the tool save or upload anything?+

    No. The model runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you click, rotate, or select is ever sent to a server, logged, or stored.

    Can I use this on my phone or tablet?+

    Yes. One finger rotates and tilts, pinching zooms, and tapping selects — so the whole model works the same with touch as it does with a mouse.

    Is this interactive human ear model free to use?+

    Yes, it's completely free with no account, no sign-up, and no limit on how much you explore.
