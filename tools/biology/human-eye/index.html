03 — HOW IT WORKS

Three ideas that run your vision

Reading the diagram is easier once you know what it's really showing. These are the three ideas the whole picture hangs on.

The optical path

light → cornea → aqueous → lens → vitreous → retina

The cornea does about two-thirds of the bending; the lens fine-tunes the rest. Rays from one point on an object cross back to a single point on the retina — flipped upside down, as the little arrow in the diagram shows.

Accommodation

contract ciliary → slack zonules → round lens → sharp retina

Your lens hangs in a muscular hammock. For near work the ciliary ring tightens, the suspension goes loose, and the lens bulges into a stronger shape. For distance, everything relaxes and the lens is pulled flat. Try it with the focus control above.

The blind spot

optic disc → 0 photoreceptors → brain fills in

Where a million fibres exit the eye, there's nothing left to catch light — yet you've never noticed a hole in your vision. That's your brain stitching over the gap, using the other eye and educated guesswork.

How do I use the interactive human eye diagram? +

Tap or click any structure — cornea, lens, retina, anything — to see its name, what it does, and a quick fact. Drag to look around, scroll or pinch to zoom, and use the quick-select list to jump straight to any part.

Is this eye diagram anatomically exact or to scale? +

No. It's a stylized cross-section drawn for clarity — a real eye is about 24 mm across, structures overlap more, and the anterior chamber is proportionally smaller. Shapes and positions follow real anatomy so everything is recognizable, but it's an illustration, not a microscope photo.

What happens when I drag the light slider? +

You're testing the pupil reflex. In dim light the iris dilator muscle pulls the pupil open to about 8 mm; in bright light the sphincter muscle squeezes it down to about 2 mm. Watch the iris fold and the live millimetre readout change as you drag.

What does switching between near and far focus show? +

Accommodation — the way your lens changes shape. For a distant object the ciliary muscle relaxes and the zonules pull the lens flat; for close work the muscle contracts, the zonules go slack, and the lens rounds up. Follow the light rays either way: they always converge into a sharp spot on the retina.

Why is the image on the retina upside down? +

Because the lens crosses light rays over — the top of any object you look at projects onto the lower retina, and vice versa. Your brain re-flips it so the world feels right-side up. The little arrow in the diagram shows the inverted image.

What is the blind spot, and where is it? +

The optic disc — the pale patch where a million nerve fibres gather to leave the eye as the optic nerve. There are no photoreceptors there, so light landing on it is simply never seen. Your brain fills the gap using the surrounding view.

Does the tool save or upload anything? +

No. The whole diagram runs in your browser. Nothing you click, zoom or slide is ever sent to a server, logged or stored.

Can I use this on my phone or tablet? Is it free? +

Yes to both. Tapping selects a structure, dragging pans the view and pinching zooms — it works the same with touch as with a mouse. And it's completely free: no account, no sign-up, no watermark, no limits.
